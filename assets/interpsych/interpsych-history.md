# InterPsych: a recovered history

Ian Pitchford's international mental-health network, from its conception in 1993 to its later dispersed life. Reconstructed from public online records, checked on 22 and 30 October 2026.

On 29 January 1994, Ian Pitchford posted a proposal to the BIONET neuroscience discussion group. Writing from Sheffield's Department of Biomedical Science, he described a gulf between biological and psychological understandings of mental illness and asked whether anyone wanted a forum in which the competing approaches could meet. This is the earliest contemporary precursor recovered in this investigation. It presents the project as an intellectual intervention: better communication might improve the quality of the field itself. [Pitchford's original proposal](http://www.bio.net/bionet/mm/neur-sci/1994-January/013263.html).

Sixteen days later, on **14 February 1994**, he announced the new, unmoderated `psychiatry@mailbase.ac.uk` list. Its invitation ranged across research methods, clinical practice, philosophy, psychiatric classification and the experience of mental illness. Biomedical, existential and psychodynamic perspectives were explicitly invited into conversation. The announcement already carried the principle that would become InterPsych's motto: **“There is someone wiser than any of us, and that is all of us.”** [Launch-day announcement](http://www.bio.net/bionet/mm/neur-sci/1994-February/013377.html).

The familiar uncertainty between 1993 and 1994 can therefore be substantially resolved. The organisation's Sheffield homepage, preserved in a December 1998 capture, dates Pitchford's conception of the project to **December 1993**. The operational launch is documented contemporaneously in February 1994. In May, the first newsletter announced that the board had decided to name the expanding group of lists **InterPsych**. The earlier name **PsychNet** survives in its text and in a May announcement. These are stages of the same development, rather than necessarily contradictory founding dates. [Sheffield homepage](https://web.archive.org/web/19981203090622/http://www.shef.ac.uk:80/uni/projects/gpp/index.html), [first newsletter](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn1_1.htm), [21 May announcement](http://www.bio.net/bionet/mm/neur-sci/1994-May/014099.html).

The contemporary record also discourages a simple claim that InterPsych invented online mental-health discussion. A response to Pitchford's January proposal pointed him to the existing `sci.med.psychobiology` newsgroup. What became distinctive was the attempt to organise many specialised conversations into a free, international, interdisciplinary association, supported by a common identity and services. That is a more precise description of the innovation than the later homepage's promotional claim to be the world's first and largest mental-health cyberorganisation. [John M. Price's response](http://www.bio.net/bionet/mm/neur-sci/1994-January/013265.html).

The original infrastructure was email. Mailbase, based at Newcastle and serving the UK academic network, distributed a subscriber's message to everyone on the relevant list. Members did not need a modern website or a permanent connection to take part. Pitchford's recruitment text explained modems, university access and commercial email services alongside the intellectual mission. By May/June 1994 it described an international membership drawn from anthropology, computer science, neuroscience, pharmacology, philosophy, psychiatry, psychology and sociology. The central coordinating list was accompanied by groups on attachment, transcultural psychology, depression, helplessness, traumatic stress, assessment and resources. [Recruitment message, posted 3 June and signed May 1994](https://unauthorised.org/anthropology/anthro-l/june-1994/0057.html).

The movement from one conversation to a network happened quickly. The June newsletter counted **1,993 subscriptions across eight lists on 4 June**, increasing to **2,250 on 21 June**; three additional groups had just opened. It explicitly warned that subscriptions were not unique people: some belonged to several lists, while redistribution services reached readers absent from the direct totals. Argentina and Brazil were already part of this wider circulation. These qualifications matter. The surviving figures establish rapid growth, but they cannot be converted into a clean graph of unique membership. [June 1994 newsletter](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn1_2.htm).

A striking feature was the mixture of institutional seniority and practical initiative. Pitchford was identified as a Sheffield neuroscience student and founder/coordinator; Charles Figley chaired the early board, and Martin E. P. Seligman became founding president. Seligman's own university CV dates that presidency to 1994–95. Other early participants included Thomas Joiner, David DiLalla, Greg Berns and Edward Wakeman; the expanding June board also included Stevan Harnad, Ivan Goldberg, Juan Carlos Garelli and Beth Hudnall Stamm. A student-led project had assembled an international scientific and clinical network within months. [Early recruitment board](https://unauthorised.org/anthropology/anthro-l/june-1994/0057.html), [June board](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn1_2.htm), [Seligman's CV](https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/seligmancv_0.pdf).

The newsletter helped turn a collection of discussions into a community. According to the surviving editorial history, Pitchford edited its first three issues alone. From August 1994, Sean Sullivan, Sunkyo Kwon, Joseph Plaud and John Grohol formed the continuing editorial core, with several other volunteers. The volume changed from 1 to 2 to mark that transition. Its reports let members learn what other groups were discussing, discover jobs and conferences, find collaborators and follow the organisation's development. It was simultaneously a publication and a means of keeping a dispersed association in touch. [Editorial history](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn.htm).

There were practical examples of what geographical reach meant. In November 1994, Stamm reported that the new Rural-Care forum had attracted more than 180 people in two weeks, including people serving isolated areas in Alaska, Australia, KwaZulu-Natal and northern Canada. Meanwhile the network was trying to organise itself: Charles Stinson chaired its Strategic Planning Committee, Pitchford served as executive secretary, and legal advisers were assisting incorporation. Volunteers were sought to build databases and an InterPsych Virtual Campus. The campus was an announced ambition; the evidence recovered does not establish that the full proposed system was completed. [November 1994 organisational report](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn2_2_b.htm).

Messages that Pitchford sent to other academic mailing lists show the organising work of the second half of 1994. A 22 July update reported more than 2,500 members and listed the forums and the board. Between 15 and 26 August he announced new Mailbase lists for psychoanalysis, forensic psychiatry and emergency psychiatry and a current-issues forum, each with named list owners; a clinical-psychophysiology list followed on 9 September. In September he also circulated a call for volunteers to gather data for the planned InterPsych Virtual Campus, and on 3 October he forwarded the newsletter's request for editors and contributors. By 16 November a roster titled *InterPsych Resources* claimed more than 7,000 members and set out the forums, coordinators, directors and services. Like the newsletter's subscription counts, these are the organisation's own figures, not counts of distinct people. One participant's view survives from the same month: in the ASCAP newsletter, the psychiatrist Russell Gardner wrote that he had been involved for several months in InterPsych's Depression, Helplessness and Psychiatry lists. [July update](https://ksi.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/PCP/1994Q3/0031.html), [August announcements](https://ksi.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/PCP/1994Q3/0071.html), [Virtual Campus call](https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/ejournals/vpiej-l/vpiej-l.log9409.html), [request for editors](https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/ejournals/vpiej-l/vpiej-l.log9410.html), [November roster](https://ksi.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/PCP/1994Q4/0132.html), [Gardner's column](https://johnscottprice.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/ascap-vol-7-no-011.pdf).

Growth soon produced an institutional problem. On **23 September 1994**, Pitchford appealed urgently to UK members, reporting that membership had reached 6,000 and that Mailbase support was threatened. The August–October newsletter supplied more specific figures: 6,144 memberships, of which 5,825 were outside the UK. In the newsletter's account, the host considered the limited British share and transatlantic traffic difficult to justify. The very internationalism that made InterPsych valuable therefore challenged the assumptions of its original national academic host. The last sentence is a historical interpretation of the documented dispute, not a quotation from Mailbase. [Pitchford's appeal](https://ksi.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/PCP/1994Q3/0121.html), [contemporary newsletter account](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn2_1.htm).

InterPsych chose the commercial provider Netcom as an interim home. A December report anticipated a largely transparent transfer before the end of the year. The March 1995 follow-up told a different story: all Mailbase connections had ended on **22 January 1995**, and moving between incompatible systems had interrupted forums, generated unwanted mail and overwhelmed a small group of volunteers. It also prompted technical improvements to distribution and subscription management. These reports document how much work lay behind a service that members had previously experienced as automatic. [December migration plan](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn2_3_b.htm), [March account of the migration](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn2_4_a.htm).

By March 1995, membership rules were contentious. The joining guide listed open forums alongside groups requiring verified professional or student status; other applicants could also make a written case for admission. The stated rationale was that some subjects benefited from a more restricted audience. Newsletter correspondence challenged exclusions, including the position of experienced participants without the specified credentials. InterPsych consequently contained a persistent tension: an aspiration to knowledge across boundaries, alongside demands for professional confidence and controlled access. Neither a description of it as entirely open nor one of it as uniformly closed fits the surviving rules. [Joining guide](https://ksi.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/PCP/1995Q1/0217.html), [admission policy](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn2_4_b.htm), [readers' objections](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn2_4_h.htm).

The New York incorporation was itself contested. Official records identify **INTERPSYCH, INC.**, entity number **1906508**, incorporated on **23 March 1995** as a domestic not-for-profit corporation, with Benjamin I. Goldhagen named as executive director in the filing contact. [Official filing history](https://data.ny.gov/resource/63wc-4exh.json?corpid_num=1906508), [entity record](https://data.ny.gov/resource/n9v6-gdp6.json?dos_id=1906508).

Young's near-contemporary account supplies further detail about the confrontation. In a talk delivered in November 1995 and revised in January 1996, he described helping Pitchford during a dispute over an administrator's concentration of organisational and server control. He recalled a vote, an attempted reversal and a face-to-face meeting during the American Psychological Association convention in New York, after which authority returned to forum leaders. This is a supportive participant's account, not neutral minutes. Young does not name the administrator. The entity remains listed in the state's active-corporation dataset; neither that listing nor Young's description of an organisational settlement establishes the legal outcome of Pitchford and Young's challenge or present-day network activity. [Young, “Psychoanalysis and/of the Internet”](https://www.psychoanalysis-and-therapy.com/human_nature/papers/paper36h.html), [current entity record](https://data.ny.gov/resource/n9v6-gdp6.json?dos_id=1906508).

Other documents show increasing decentralisation without requiring acceptance of every detail of that account. By August–September 1995, Assessment-Psychometrics reported moving independently from Netcom to St John's University. Later bylaws, on a page dated October 1998, describe a federation in which forum leaders elect the board and president, while forums retain authority over their location and operation. These rules should not be backdated to the 1995 dispute. Read together, the sources support a move toward explicit autonomy for constituent communities, though they do not establish the complete sequence of decisions. [Forum migration report](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn2_7b.htm), [archived bylaws](https://web.archive.org/web/20000308001353/http://www.interpsych.org/ipbylaws.html).

Pitchford's ambition widened during this period. On **15 October 1995**, he announced the organising effort for the **GlobalPsych Institute**, incorporating InterPsych and a new European division, EuroPsych, into a broader proposed confederation covering health, behaviour, cognition and education. More than 150 people had joined its charter discussion. The message described proposed publishing and research activities, Human Nature Trust sponsorship, and offers of computing facilities from several institutions. It is evidence of a substantial organising initiative, not proof that every proposal became an operating programme or that a statutory corporate merger occurred. [GlobalPsych charter announcement](https://ksi.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/PCP/1995Q4/0094.html).

Electronic publishing was one of the network's most consequential ambitions. In July 1995, Goldberg argued for rapid, signed responses to research and electronic articles that could be revised as evidence developed. This was an invited editorial, not a binding organisational policy. A concrete publication followed in the wider network: the **International Journal of Psychopathology, Psychopharmacology and Psychotherapy**, published by PsyCom.Net. Its 1996 masthead explicitly identifies PsyCom.Net's affiliation with InterPsych and credits Pitchford as InterPsych's founder. Its first volume included Seymour Fisher's essays previously circulated through the psychopharmacology forum—an example of discussion-list material becoming a more formal publication. [Goldberg's editorial](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn2_6a.htm), [journal masthead](https://web.archive.org/web/19970706041427/http://www.psycom.net:80/ijppp.mast.html), [Fisher's original article](https://web.archive.org/web/19970706042145/http://www.psycom.net:80/fisher.html).

The trauma community provides particularly concrete evidence of activity beyond discussion. In June 1995, Figley reported that the Traumatic-Stress Forum and InterPsych were founding sponsors of **Operation Healing**, launched on 26 April following the Oklahoma City bombing. The report describes a way of coordinating offers of professional help and places the initiative under the emerging Green Cross banner. It also reports the first issue of **Traumatology**. These are specific organisational contributions; they do not, by themselves, demonstrate treatment outcomes or make every later Green Cross activity an InterPsych programme. [Figley's contemporary report](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn/ipn2_5c1.htm).

The journal and the forum maintained an identifiable relationship. In an editorial signed 30 December 1997, Figley called the Traumatic-Stress Forum, an InterPsych member, the journal's primary sponsor and described circulating preprints for discussion. A later editorial traces the journal's subsequent publishing history. There is a metadata trap here: the online upload of the earlier editorial is labelled as a 1995 inaugural note, but its actual text is from 1997. The relationship is documented; the upload label should not determine the date. [Actual 1997 editorial in an author upload](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325455078_1995_Inaugurall_Editorial_Note_in_Traumatology_V1_i2), [2006 publishing-history editorial](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/153476560601200101?download=true).

A 1999 article by Joyce Carbonell and Figley supplies another trace: its authors describe discussions in the trauma forum leading to the selection of treatments for a clinical demonstration, with nominations solicited from InterPsych. Their reported 10,000-person mailing should be treated as a retrospective reach claim, not a verified membership count. The bibliography dates the survey to January–May 1994, which is difficult to reconcile with the February launch and much smaller June subscription totals. The paper nevertheless documents a specific use of the network in organising research. It does not establish the effectiveness of the treatments considered. [Article text](https://www.psychotherapy-center.com/Article-FigleyResearchPTSD99.pdf), [publisher record](https://sage.cnpereading.com/doi/10.1177/153476569900500106).

The forums were also a route for recruiting research participants. A poster on compassion fatigue, presented by Joseph Rudolph, B. Hudnall Stamm and Henry Stamm at the International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies meeting in Montreal in November 1997, drew 19 of its 179 respondents from InterPsych. A 1999 study by J. Michael Bailey and Amy Shriver recruited clinical psychologists through InterPsych's clinical psychology list, which it described as having about 600 members; 22 people responded and 19 returned questionnaires. These are particular studies' sampling routes, not measures of the network's size. [1997 poster](https://web.archive.org/web/20000824054106id_/http://www.isu.edu:80/~bhstamm/pdf/ISTSS97cf.pdf), [Bailey and Shriver](https://doi.org/10.1080/00926239908403976).

There were other forms of professional uptake. A 1995 American Psychiatric Association programme advertised an InterPsych demonstration by Russell Lim and Charles Stinson; Leslie Knowlton's September 1996 reporting describes Lim presenting it at the association's annual meeting. Ben Green's January 1996 *Psychiatric Bulletin* guide also treated it as a substantial resource. InterPsych had become something clinicians were taught to use, not simply a project its own members discussed. [1995 programme](https://www.psychiatry.org/getattachment/aae86b0e-ee46-4a48-b1dd-801adb027eb2/ips_syllabus_1995.pdf), [Knowlton's report](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/computer-coverage-nets-capacity-crowds), [Green's article](https://doi.org/10.1192/pb.20.1.36).

Two 1996 journal articles described the network to clinicians. In the *Psychiatric Bulletin*, Carl Littlejohns and Martin Briscoe outlined its lists, its electronically distributed journal and the plan for a virtual campus; in the *American Journal of Psychiatry*, Milton Huang and Norman Alessi listed its psychopharmacology list and a “Staff Lounge” chat channel. A year earlier Peter Mezciems had introduced the substance-related disorders forum to readers of the American Society of Addiction Medicine's newsletter. InterPsych's traffic also registered in wider internet administration: a 1995 proposal to reorganise the `sci.psychology` newsgroups gave the high volume of some InterPsych mailing lists as a reason for new groups. That shows the lists were visible beyond their members, not that InterPsych controlled the newsgroups or how its members voted. [Littlejohns and Briscoe](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8B8B61794F894DEE956F6F8A5A20C796/S0955603600105434a.pdf/div-class-title-the-information-superhighway-and-psychiatry-div.pdf), [Huang and Alessi](https://www.emotrics.com/people/milton/papers/netfuturepsych/), [Mezciems](https://downloads.asam.org/sitefinity-production-blobs/docs/default-source/publications/asam-news-archives/1990s/1995-8-9vol10-4ocr.pdf?sfvrsn=5db5bc2_0), [newsgroup proposal](https://ftp.isc.org/pub/usenet/news.announce.newgroups/sci/sci.psychology-reorg).

The newsletter meanwhile acquired a separate life. The archive's editorial history dates the decision to continue independently as **PsychNews International** to December 1995; the first surviving issue under that title is dated April 1996. It explicitly tells former InterPsych Newsletter subscribers about the change. The University of Wisconsin's *Scout Report* noticed the renamed publication on 31 May. This was an editorial separation with continuity of contributors and readers, not evidence that InterPsych had ceased to exist. [Archive history](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/ipn.htm), [April 1996 introduction](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/1_1/pni1_1a.htm), [external notice](https://scout.wisc.edu/report/1996/0531).

By 1998, participants offered markedly different assessments of what the association had achieved. John Grohol, writing about the new International Society for Mental Health Online, described InterPsych's organisational success as limited. He pointed to vague goals, restrictions on membership and the difficulty of coordinating independent, asynchronous lists across professions. This is an informed participant's criticism, made while advocating another organisation. It does not show that ISMHO was a legal or organisational continuation of InterPsych. The preserved issue misprints its year as 1997; staff notices and the essay's events establish that it belongs to March 1998. [Grohol's assessment](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/3_1/pn3_1_3.htm), [issue notices](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/3_1/pn3_1_1.htm).

Steven Dubovsky's contribution to the same issue gives a more clinically grounded assessment. He valued the reach of online consultation while describing risks arising from inadequate case descriptions, confidentiality problems, advice without follow-up and confident recommendations whose evidence was uncertain. The significance of this criticism is historical: InterPsych's participants were already confronting problems that arise when professional conversation becomes fast, persistent and geographically unrestricted. It would be wrong to turn their exchange of ideas into an unqualified claim of improved patient care. [Dubovsky's editorial](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/3_1/pn3_1_2.htm).

The St John's hosting story offers a different view of survival. In the July 1998 tribute to Robert Zenhausern, Dubovsky credited him with helping the forums move beyond Netcom. Zenhausern reported more than 45 associated lists, over half hosted at Maelstrom, and acknowledged Pitchford's 1993 conception. The much larger figures elsewhere in that tribute describe the whole St John's mailing-list service, not InterPsych. Its technical patronage mattered: the network's endurance depended on donated infrastructure and people who maintained it, as well as its founders and prominent participants. [Zenhausern and Dubovsky's accounts](https://userpage.fu-berlin.de/~expert/psychnews/3_2/pn3_2_5.htm).

A fuller picture of the forum system survives in the Sheffield copy of the InterPsych directory, preserved in captures from December 1998 to April 1999. Its 44 forum pages name coordinators and describe each forum's purpose, host and admission arrangements, from addiction, affective disorders and attachment to thanatology, transcultural psychology and youth anxiety and depression. Some forums were open to the public and others were restricted to professionals; Latin Psych gave Spanish, Portuguese and English as its main languages. The captures show when the pages were preserved, not that every forum was active then. [Sheffield directory](https://web.archive.org/web/19990203121309/http://www.shef.ac.uk/%7Epsysc/InterPsych/inter.html), [Latin Psych](https://web.archive.org/web/19990203134020/http://www.shef.ac.uk:80/%7Epsysc/InterPsych/latin-psych.html).

There is no defensible single closing date in the records recovered. A directory at `interpsych.net`, captured in August 2001, carries an update date of **10 November 2000** and lists 27 forum headings. Several individual entries were revised in 2000. It includes telehealth, public mental health, rural care, trauma, transcultural psychology and more established subjects. The entries do not establish how active each forum remained. Its 27 headings cannot simply be compared with earlier totals to calculate decline, because the scope and units of the lists differ. [Directory updated in November 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/20010815002821/http://www.interpsych.net:80/inter.htm).

An International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies bulletin, datable by its contents to around 2001, still places two co-sponsored forums within InterPsych and names Figley as president. An Idaho State directory carries a November 2001 update, and a gateway page preserved in 2005 has an InterPsych-specific update from May 2003. These are different strengths of evidence: a report of affiliation, a maintained directory, and a surviving gateway page. Domain parking and old university mirrors establish the fate of particular websites, not the date the association or all its forums stopped operating. [ISTSS bulletin](https://istss.org/communications-corner-istss-18/), [Idaho directory](https://web.archive.org/web/20021201034922/http://www.isu.edu:80/~bhstamm/ip/inter.htm), [Idaho gateway](https://web.archive.org/web/20051125134953/http://www.isu.edu:80/%7Ebhstamm/InterPsych.htm).

Some influence can be followed into later literature. A 1998 telemedicine poster describes drawing on InterPsych's distributed-information model in designing administration for a multisite clinical trial. A 2005 author manuscript by Stamm and Cunningham remembers it as an early experiment in distributed professional education; this passage needs to be attributed to that manuscript because versions of the published article differ. In a separate line of influence, a 2005 *BMJ* editorial cites Michael J. Cohen's 1995 InterPsych Newsletter article on counselling and nature. These are specific traces of circulation and reuse, not a claim that the network caused all later developments in telehealth or ecotherapy. [Telemedicine poster](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=a81f51119086160b76b3db82a473798f244d9b10&repid=rep1&type=pdf), [education manuscript](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7529110_The_education_part_of_telehealth), [BMJ editorial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1289309/).

The social legacy is harder to measure but not wholly lost. Philip Brownell's retrospective account of the beginnings of *Gestalt!* recalls meeting Pitchford through the early online world and credits his creation of InterPsych. German handbooks, a French psychiatric resource commentary and an international missionary-care bibliography also recognised the network. These accounts show that its reputation crossed national and professional boundaries. They require careful reading: the German chapter gives an unsupported 1992 founding date, and the French author openly admits uncertainty about the forums' later status. A citation can preserve a reputation long after it ceases to describe current operations. [Brownell's publisher sample](https://www.cambridgescholars.com/resources/pdfs/978-1-4438-7079-5-sample.pdf), [German handbook chapter](https://www.andre-hahn.de/downloads/pub/2000/2000b_Psychologie_im_Internet.pdf), [French account](https://www.bulletindepsychiatrie.com/sites.htm), [international resource bibliography](https://missionexus.org/innovate/library/emq/touring-the-terrain-an-international-sampler-of-member-care-literature/).

A retrospective statement of one role survives from 2006. Declaring his interests in a journal article, the psychiatrist Digby Tantam wrote that he had been a director of InterPsych, “the network of discussion forums on mental health created by Ian Pitchford”. He gives no dates for the directorship. [Tantam's declaration](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8C39F32709748E1AE1976AC35A59AF91/S1355514600003382a.pdf/div-class-title-the-machine-as-intermediary-personal-communication-via-a-machine-div.pdf).

My interpretation of this evidence is that InterPsych's durable contribution lay in creating relationships and working arrangements that could survive changes of host, title and institutional umbrella. Its central organisation encountered serious limitations, acknowledged by its own participants. Yet the original wish to bring competing approaches into conversation became a practical network, produced publications, supported research and disaster-response organising, and provided an early model of distributed professional education. Its significance is visible in those documented activities and connections; it does not depend on accepting its most expansive promotional claims.

This reconstruction began with a broad public-web and archive investigation. It does not claim to have exhausted the internet or recovered private archival correspondence. The searchable FU Berlin collection comprises 310 retrieved HTML pages, including navigation and duplicate issue sections, from 329 attempted URLs. Additional research used original mailing-list messages, Wayback snapshots, official records, professional journalism, scholarly material and institutional histories. The accompanying catalogue distinguishes contemporary records and later recollections. Unresolved questions concern the first private discussions in 1993, the complete record and legal outcome of the 1995 dispute, the extent of realised GlobalPsych projects, and when particular forums or central coordinating activities ended. A further search on 30 October 2026 added 62 source records and the 44 forum pages of the archived Sheffield directory; every added link was retrieved and checked on that date, and leads that could not be verified are listed separately in the research notes. No formal interviews were conducted. AI-assisted research and synthesis were checked against the linked records.
