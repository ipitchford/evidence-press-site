# Research scope and evidence guide

This investigation reconstructs Ian Pitchford’s InterPsych from publicly accessible online sources, checked on 22 October 2026, and a founder clarification supplied in this conversation on the same date. It covers conception, launch, naming, expansion, governance, hosting, publications, reception and later traces. It is a broad historical investigation, not a claim that every online resource or private document has been recovered.

The source catalogue contains 112 source records: 111 unique online document URLs and one dated first-person clarification (T01), drawn from 137 annotated entries. Duplicate URLs have been merged; aliases preserve the original research identifiers. Different mirrors and publications by the same people remain related evidence, not independent witnesses. The dated chronology has 36 entries. The people index is selective and its roles refer to the stated historical dates, not current appointments.

Searches used ordinary web search, Exa, BIONET/Usenet and university mailing-list archives, FU Berlin’s newsletter collection, Wayback CDX indexes and actual captures, professional journalism, conference programmes, scholarly publications, author manuscripts, CVs, library catalogues and New York public records. Search terms combined InterPsych/PsychNet/GlobalPsych with founders, forum leaders, dates, hosting, incorporation, newsletter, dispute, closure, journal, trauma, telehealth and later citations. English, German, French and other international references were followed when found. Similar-name clinics, employment-services businesses and modern companies were excluded.

The bounded FU Berlin retrieval attempted 329 URLs and retrieved 310 HTML pages. This includes frames, indexes, duplicated issue/section presentations and unrelated resources. It is not that many independent sources or a claim every page was closely read. Four concurrent requests, a 20-second timeout, maximum 400 URLs and maximum depth eight were used; traversal ended naturally at depth three. Full-text searches identified passages for close reading.

The separate historical-site recovery saved 44 HTML pages and matching text files, plus archive indexes and retrieval logs. Searches included Sheffield, St John’s, Michigan, InterPsych.org, InterPsych.net, Idaho State, FSU, schizophrenia.com and Dartmouth host paths. The inventory includes retrieved pages and failures; checksum fields identify captured bytes, not the historical truth of a page. Full source texts remain working evidence and are not reproduced in this deliverable.

Dated original messages anchor the founding chronology. Newsletter reports establish what the organisation publicly reported; subscriptions and reach figures remain attributed. Later official histories, CVs and memoirs help identify roles but can misremember dates. Independent professional reporting demonstrates public visibility. Government records establish a corporation’s filing history, not an active professional network. A website capture can preserve old material, so capture dates and internal dates are separated.

Access limitations remain visible in the catalogue. The PsychiatryOnline 1995 PDF returned 403; indexed passages were available. The FSU historical supplement returned 404, and Brownell’s publisher sample returned generic HTML; indexed excerpts supplied limited evidence. The CiteSeerX poster was inspected through indexed content, not a successfully downloaded PDF. Semantic Scholar returned a rate limit; Scite required a paid subscription. WorldCat, Library Hub and UK Web Archive searches were incomplete or unavailable. Negative results do not prove that these services hold nothing relevant.

Known date conflicts include a later unsupported 1992 foundation claim; March versus April 1994 for the traumatic-stress forum; a 1997 masthead on a PsychNews issue whose contents establish March 1998; and a ResearchGate upload labelled 1995 whose actual editorial is signed December 1997. The source catalogue records these rather than silently choosing search-engine dates.

The 1995 governance confrontation is documented through Robert M. Young’s supportive eyewitness account and Pitchford’s clarification supplied on 22 October 2026. Pitchford identifies the New York incorporation by Goldhagen as the matter he and Young travelled to New York to contest. This corrects the original draft’s separation of incorporation from the dispute: the filing is no longer presented as an agreed formalisation of the network. The public record establishes the filing date; the founder’s testimony identifies the object of their challenge. No original minutes, opposing account, vote tallies or legal disposition of that challenge were recovered. Young’s text still does not name its administrator, and the revision does not automatically attribute all his further allegations to Goldhagen. The later bylaws are not backdated to the confrontation, and the newsletter’s independence is not attributed to it without evidence. No formal legal merger into GlobalPsych, definitive organisational closing date or complete forum-by-forum traffic history was established.

The New York corporation whose incorporation was contested, the voluntary federation, newsletter, domains, host institutions and individual forums are distinct. Their histories overlap but do not end together. Registration alone does not establish the founder’s or network’s consent. Announced projects are separated from outputs actually recovered. Clinical and research activity is described historically; treatment efficacy is not inferred.

AI-assisted researchers collected and compared the evidence, and an internal AI review checked the initial draft. The founder’s subsequent clarification was incorporated with targeted consistency checks across the deliverables; no fresh external review is claimed. No formal interviews, private archival correspondence, purchases or external outreach were used. The founder’s supplied statement is recorded separately as T01. No material has been published to an external website. The evidence remains open to further correction by original documents or first-person recollections.
