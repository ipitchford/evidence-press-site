Picture a network shaped like a tree, with branches but no loops, and add up the distances between every pair of its points. A chemist introduced this total to predict the boiling points of fuel molecules, and today it is a standard measure of how spread out a network is.

Suppose you are told the diameter, the longest distance anywhere in the tree. No distance can be longer, so the average falls below the diameter. The question is how far below it must fall for the most spread-out tree.

Earlier work by Stijn Cambie showed how this shortfall grows and pinned its key constant between about zero point four seven one and zero point four eight three, a gap of roughly two and a half percent. The exact value was left open.

This release settles it. The constant is about zero point four seven seven five, an exact expression involving the square root of seven, and it holds whenever the diameter grows but stays small compared with the size of the tree. The best trees have a striking shape: each branch splits into two equal halves, again and again, and each new split comes when only a quarter as much height remains as at the split before.

For every fixed diameter, the paper also finds the next two terms, computed from the electrical resistance of small trees. Large best trees are built from a finite catalogue of branch shapes, diameter five is solved completely, and exact computer searches on trees of up to three thousand points show where the catalogue takes over.

The proof treats the leaves as an electric current flowing out from the centre, so the cost of a branch becomes the energy of that current.

The results are about trees, not general networks, and finer corrections remain open.
