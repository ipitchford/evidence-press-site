Same three trips, different fate. An Evidence Press briefing on tie-breaking in warehouse order picking. Picture a warehouse worker pushing a cart with two bins. Each customer order fills one bin, and new orders keep arriving. A common rule is to reoptimise: every time the list of orders changes, work out the fastest way to finish everything on it, start that plan, and throw the rest away when new orders arrive. Now suppose five orders are waiting at the same shelf. One plan takes a single order first, then two full cartloads. Another takes two full cartloads first, and the single order last. Both need three trips and finish at exactly the same time, so a solver may return either. But orders keep arriving during the first trip, and the plan is recomputed. The paper shows that this harmless-looking tie decides whether the warehouse keeps up. A picker who always takes the leftover orders first falls further and further behind once demand passes about seventy-six per cent of what the cart can carry. With eight bins that happens at about forty-nine per cent, and for very large carts at about thirty-seven per cent. A picker who always fills the cart first keeps up all the way to full capacity. The gap survives picking times and two separate shelves, and a simple tie-breaking rule, finishing orders as early as possible on average, picks the safe option. The paper also proves an open conjecture from the research that introduced these models: when orders are rare enough, reoptimisation is always stable, whatever the tie rule, although the condition it proves is cautious. The results have written proofs and computer checks, but only synthetic data, not real warehouse records.
