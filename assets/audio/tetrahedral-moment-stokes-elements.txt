Why fourth-order tetrahedral Stokes elements need a sextic. An Evidence Press briefing. Engineers simulate slow, sticky flows, such as blood in small vessels or oil in porous rock, by cutting space into small tetrahedra and approximating the flow in each one by a polynomial. The recipe for one cell is called a finite element. A popular way to keep velocity and pressure stable is to describe each cell by its moments: averages of the velocity against simple polynomials on each face and inside the cell. For this to work, the moments must pick out exactly one polynomial from the chosen space, and the space should use the lowest degree possible, because higher degree costs more computing time. In 2025, Balazi, Allaire, Jolivet and Omnes built elements of second and third order in this way. For fourth order they needed fifteen extra polynomials of degree five, tested millions of random choices, found none, and asked whether any choice exists. This paper shows that none does. On every polynomial of degree five, the fifty moments obey one hidden relation, revealed by a short symmetry argument, so they can never be independent. A single polynomial of degree six, the product of the six differences of the tetrahedron's barycentric coordinates, breaks the relation and gives a fourth-order element that does not depend on how the vertices are numbered. More generally, for every order the paper finds exactly seven polynomials that the moments cannot see. As a result, the natural degree works only for the two lowest orders and then falls short by a growing, exactly known number of directions, while one degree higher always works. The paper proves that symmetric elements with the fewest extra functions exist for every order, and shows that on triangles no extra degree is ever needed. A numerical test of the new element shows error rates consistent with fourth-order convergence. Some statements in four or more dimensions remain conjectures.
