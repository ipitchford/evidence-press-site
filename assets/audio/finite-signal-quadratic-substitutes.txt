When does more information bring diminishing returns? An Evidence Press audio briefing. Imagine predicting one numerical outcome using two reports. Each report has finitely many possible values, and prediction errors are measured by their squares. You may reveal only part of the first report, using fresh randomness. The question is whether the second report is always at least as valuable after this partial disclosure as it is after the first report has been revealed completely. There are infinitely many possible disclosure rules. This research candidate replaces that infinite test with finitely many mathematical checks. For each distinct average prediction from the first report, an auxiliary list of numbers must meet all the relevant squared-distance limits at once. These are convex feasibility problems. The result allows impossible combinations of reports; it does not assume that every combination occurs. When every combination is possible, the checks simplify to explicit comparisons between pairs of report values. But that shortcut has limits. One example with impossible combinations passes every two-state restriction and still fails when three states are mixed. Another example shows that pairwise checks can fail for a prediction with several numerical components, even when every report combination is possible. The practical use is screening an exactly specified information model, not proving that a real forecasting market will improve. Checking both disclosure directions requires applying the criterion twice. The work concerns two finite signals, one numerical target, and squared-error prediction. It does not cover arbitrary scoring rules or arbitrary pairs of partial disclosures. Written mathematical proofs support the general claims.
