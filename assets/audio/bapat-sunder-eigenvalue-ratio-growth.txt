This is an audio briefing from Evidence Press on a research candidate about how far a matrix eigenvalue can rise above a quantity called the permanent.

The permanent is the determinant's sign-free cousin: add up the products of a matrix's entries along every permutation, with no minus signs. Take a table of inner products of vectors, a positive semidefinite matrix. From the permanents of its smaller pieces one builds a companion matrix whose rows all add up to the permanent, so the permanent is one of its eigenvalues. Bapat and Sunder asked whether it is always the largest eigenvalue.

The question also has a physical meaning. For photons passing through an interferometer, the chance that they all bunch into a few output channels is a permanent, and an eigenvalue above the permanent means that small time delays between the photons can raise that chance.

Counterexamples were already known, but nobody knew how large the violation could get. For matrices of size n, the worst ratio of the top eigenvalue to the permanent grows like one half of the natural logarithm of n for real matrices, and like the full logarithm for complex matrices, with explicit bounds that match up to a smaller correction. Real matrices of rank three and complex matrices of rank two already reach these values, so three is the smallest real rank at which the conjecture fails. The paper also gives a checkable integer example of size twenty-seven, smaller than the previous record, and shows that a related inequality of Pate fails without bound for higher compounds.

For the upper bound, the proof tests the matrix on simple zero-one vectors and rebuilds every other vector from layers of those. For the lower bound, it places the roots of a polynomial on circles of growing size, and each circle adds the same amount to the eigenvalue.

The limits are clear: only the leading constants are sharp, the smallest real size of a counterexample is open, and the optics concerns an idealised model, not an experiment.

This is an unrefereed candidate, checked by internal AI-assisted review and exact computer arithmetic rather than by peer review, and this briefing uses a synthetic voice that is not additional evidence.
