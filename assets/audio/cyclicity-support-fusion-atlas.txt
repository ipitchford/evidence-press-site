A polynomial exponential period begins with a polynomial phase and an integral. Differentiate that integral repeatedly, and a differential system appears. The fixed-seed cyclic rank asks how much of that system is generated by one starting form, dx. Earlier releases showed that this rank can be read at infinity: normalise an inverse branch of the polynomial, split its Laurent series into congruence channels, and count the nonzero channels. The same number is the dimension of the reduced constant span of the polynomial's inverse roots. A Fourier formula then explains how those root spans fuse when the polynomial decomposes. Version zero point six takes the next step. Relative to named classification theorems for primitive polynomial monodromy and finite projective linear groups, the candidate classifies every complex polynomial phase whose fixed-seed cyclic rank is at most three, in every degree. Up to affine equivalence and lower-rank overlaps, the list contains powers, Dickson polynomials, quadratic and cubic polynomials over a power, indecomposable quartics, and one exceptional degree-twelve collision. It also identifies every proper outer composition that preserves rank. Powers extend through power towers. Dickson polynomials extend inside one aligned dihedral tower. An odd-centred cubic over a power admits one aligned square. The exceptional quartic admits one cubic collision. No other proper rank-preserving outer extension survives the argument. Several missing interfaces had to be closed. An algebraic invariant-integral descent replaces an analytic boundedness step. A full-block stabiliser lemma connects block fusion to absolute monodromy. A source-exact audit reduces the primitive cases to powers, Dickson factors, and quartics. A false draft claim about a uniform Kummer kernel was found on a special even-exponent coefficient stratum; the release preserves the counterexample and replaces the claim with a stratified theorem and exceptional descent. Arbitrary mixed Dickson chains are handled directly through the invariant field of the full outer polynomial. The old degree-twenty atlas and the preregistered degree-thirty-two experiment remain useful regression and bridge evidence, but neither is extrapolated into the universal proof. Producer-side checks include sixteen Lean theorem controls, ten exact global computer-algebra checks, nineteen repaired Kummer checks, thirteen adversarial receipt mutations in each Python mode, clean-archive replay, and two internal model-assisted specialist re-audits. These are serious controls, but they are not external peer review. The imported classification theorems are not reproved, the Lean development is deliberately partial, and no unaffiliated researcher has rerun or reimplemented the package. Novelty and priority searches are bounded rather than exhaustive. Why might this matter? The classification converts an unbounded search through polynomial coefficients and decomposition chains into a short structural decision tree. Researchers can read rank from inverse support, test whether a decomposition preserves rank through projected support and block fusion, and rule out every outer extension not on the four-item list. That makes the package useful both as a theorem candidate and as a benchmark for independent computer algebra, formalisation, and future work on differential-equation order.
