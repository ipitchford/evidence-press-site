When does a mathematical guarantee promise too much? This Evidence Press briefing concerns an unrefereed, AI-assisted research candidate about Douglas–Rachford splitting, a method that tackles a difficult problem through two simpler calculations.

A recent conjecture proposed a fast guarantee for the squared size of the method's updates. Roughly, doubling the iteration count should improve its worst-case bound by a factor of four, under the stated assumptions.

The paper gives two exact counterexamples. One works in a single real dimension. The other uses a two-dimensional rotation and does not require overrelaxation. Both obey the conjecture's actual conditions. Across the full stated class, the worst-case squared residual instead decreases at the order of one divided by the iteration count.

Why can this happen? The condition called cocoercivity controls a rotational component, but does not eliminate it. It is not the same as requiring a convex gradient.

There are important limits. The quantity studied is a fixed-point residual, not an objective gap or the distance to the solution. Each fixed rotational example can still converge geometrically. The worst-case construction changes with the number of iterations being tested. This is not a claim that practical solvers fail to converge.
