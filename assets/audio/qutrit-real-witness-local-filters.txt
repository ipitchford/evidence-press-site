To show that two quantum particles are entangled, experimenters measure an entanglement witness. Its average can only go negative when the particles really are entangled, so a negative reading is proof. A witness is written as a table of numbers, and in general those numbers are complex. Real numbers are simpler, and a natural hope is that nothing is lost by using only real tables, as long as each laboratory may first choose a good local basis, or apply a local filter that reshapes its own particle's state.

Shen, Chen and Bian conjectured exactly this, in two forms: one allowing only changes of basis, and one also allowing local filters. They proved it for many states, and it holds automatically for the smallest systems.

This release shows that both forms are false. It writes down an explicit state of two three-level systems that a complex witness detects, while every real witness misses it, whatever local filter is applied first. The same holds for every state close enough to it. The basis-change form already fails for a qubit paired with a four-level system, the smallest size where it could fail. The paper also gives a rule for every size: the conjecture holds exactly when every corner of the set of witnesses can be approached by filtered real ones. And one extra local qubit is enough to restore detection.

The idea is to take a witness that touches the unentangled states at a single point. Nudge that point slightly towards entanglement, and the witness detects the result while every filtered real witness misses it. To show that this witness is not secretly real, the paper uses a polynomial that local filters can only rescale by a positive factor, and that stays real for real tables. For this witness, the polynomial is not real, so the witness lies outside the real class.

There are limits. The explicit state sits extremely close to the unentangled ones, so this is a mathematical statement about which measurements suffice, not a proposal for an experiment. The witness and the unentangled state come from earlier work by Ha and Kye. Whether the filter form also fails for a qubit with a four-level system is still open. Experiments can still detect such states by combining several real measurements with classical processing.
