When one split-network design wins. An Evidence Press briefing. Imagine a network whose links survive independently. Success here does not mean keeping everyone connected. It means exactly two connected groups, with one designated terminal in each and no third group. For a fixed number of vertices and edges, can one design maximise that success probability at every link-survival rate? This candidate gives an almost complete existence classification for networks with at least seven vertices. Trees have a uniformly best design, as earlier work established. A substantial intermediate range has none. Sufficiently dense networks have an explicit best construction. Having no uniform winner does not mean there is no best design at a particular survival rate. Two boundary edge counts remain conditional in general. Exact integer certificates settle both for networks from seven through fifty-one vertices. That finite conclusion is separate from a new all-order comparison: it works when links survive with probability at least one half, and on an explicit interval near zero, but leaves the middle unresolved. The proof connects split reliability to ordinary connectivity after identifying the terminals. Endpoint comparisons then reveal when one design cannot win everywhere.
