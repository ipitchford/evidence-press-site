Do the edge-containing sets of a graph spread out like coin tosses? An Evidence Press briefing. Take any network of points joined by links, what mathematicians call a graph. Pick some of its points, and call your choice dependent if it includes both ends of at least one link. For each size k, count the dependent choices of k points. In 2002 David Horrocks proved that these counts are log-concave: they rise and then fall smoothly, with no second hump. In 2026 Yan-Ting Xie and Shou-Jun Xu conjectured something stronger. Divide each count by the total number of ways to choose k points, so that you get the fraction of k-point choices that are dependent. They conjectured that this sequence of fractions is still log-concave. This is called ultra log-concavity, and it means that the size of a random dependent choice is at least as tightly concentrated as the number of heads in a run of coin tosses. They had proved it only for two special families of graphs. This paper proves it for every graph, and more: each point may carry a weight, and single points may also count as dependent on their own. The key step is a new fact about splitting the points into two groups so that each group contains a link: after the same division, the number of such splits rises and falls symmetrically. A classical counting theorem of Katona and a criterion of Kahn and Neiman then finish the argument. The paper shows the condition is sharp: if a dependent choice must contain three linked points instead of two, the property can fail, with ninety-six falling short of one hundred and twenty in a five-point example. It also settles a broader conjecture of Xie and Xu for small cases, while the general case remains open. A practical consequence is a variance bound for random sets conditioned to contain a link.
