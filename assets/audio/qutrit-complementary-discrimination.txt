Can two quantum measurements be trusted to count correlation? An Evidence Press briefing. Some quantum measurements are complementary, like position and momentum: if one gives a predictable answer, the other cannot. In 2014, Schneeloch, Broadbent and Howell proposed that if two parties each make two complementary measurements on a shared system, the correlations in their two tables of results can never add up to more than the total correlation in the system. That would make two tables a cheap, guaranteed lower bound. In 2026 the idea was shown to fail in most dimensions, first in public code repositories by Ethan O'Connor and by AlperTheKing, then by Wang, Wang and Chen, and by Qian. This paper answers two follow-up questions. The first concerns a three-level system, a qutrit. How well can two complementary measurements tell two perfectly distinguishable qutrit states apart? On a natural score where four is a trivial ceiling, the paper proves that the best possible score is exactly eight thirds, and it finds every case where that best score is reached, linked to a famous symmetric arrangement called the Hesse configuration. One consequence: measuring this way recovers at most two thirds of the available information in a simple estimation task. The proof is computer-assisted: an exact certificate in whole-number fractions, checked by a short program that anyone can rerun. The second result concerns a qubit paired with a system of dimension four or more. There, every choice of complementary measurements is fooled by some unentangled state. Before, this was known only from dimension one hundred and sixty-four. The violations are tiny, so this is a structural limit, not an experimental one. The question for a qubit paired with a qutrit stays open, as does the exact best score in dimension five.
