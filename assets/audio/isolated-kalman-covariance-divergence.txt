Can a team of robots trust the uncertainty it reports? An Evidence Press briefing. When several robots or sensors track a shared state, an ordinary Kalman filter would keep one large table of how uncertain every quantity is and how the errors are linked. Sending that table around is expensive. The isolated Kalman filter, published by Jung, Luft and Weiss in Autonomous Robots in 2025, saves communication. When a measurement involves a few units, only those units update properly, and their links to everyone else are approximated by a simple correction. Its authors conjectured that, whenever the system can be observed and controlled, the uncertainty this filter reports always settles to one finite, stable value. This paper shows that it does not. Take three units, and let each measurement see the sum of two of their states. The system is observable and controllable, and every local step of the filter stays valid. Yet with states that triple at every step, the reported variances grow without limit: they are proved to be at least five quarters times nine fifths to the power k, minus one quarter, after k rounds. The ordinary, centralised Kalman filter on the same system keeps every variance at or below three quarters. The failure also appears with much milder growth. Computer-assisted proofs show that large starting uncertainty escapes when the growth per step is only slightly above one, and that at one growth rate a small start settles while a large start explodes, so no single steady value attracts every start. There are honest limits. In the tests, the filter's actual error stayed bounded; what fails is the reported uncertainty, which becomes wildly pessimistic and stops meaning anything. Stable systems are proved never to diverge, and anchored relative measurements stay safe. This is an unrefereed candidate, checked by internal AI-assisted review only.
