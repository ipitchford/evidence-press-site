Rare rich patches give population curves many peaks. An Evidence Press briefing. First, the background: imagine a species spread over a patchy landscape, where individuals leave their spot at some rate and land anywhere in the landscape with equal chance. Ecologists have long asked how the total population depends on that movement rate. In 2021, Xueli Bai and Fang Li proved that, in this well-mixed model, the total has a single peak whenever habitat quality varies by less than a factor of about one point six, and they asked whether it could ever have more than one. Now, the main finding: it can, and in any number. For every number of peaks, there are smooth habitats, arbitrarily close to uniform on average, whose total population rises and falls that many times as movement changes. The trick is a tiny patch of very rich habitat. It barely changes average quality, but it adds a small population bump at a movement rate comparable to its richness. Two habitat levels always give one peak; three are enough for two, and an exact example is certified. As for how large the effect is: the peaks are real but tiny. In one example the extra peak is about ten to the minus thirty-two high, and it vanishes if the numbers are nudged. So the result is qualitative. As for how sure this is: every theorem has a written proof, and the key identities are checked in exact arithmetic. Finally, the limits: review so far is internal and AI-assisted, and two closely related papers were read only as abstracts, so priority is stated with care.
