When we forecast two things at once, such as the wind at two nearby sites, getting each forecast's range right still leaves open how the two move together. A standard way to score such forecasts, the energy score, rests on one number: how far apart two independent draws are on average, with each distance raised to a chosen power. So a natural question is how large that number can be when both individual distributions are fixed and only the way they depend on each other is free. The answer bounds how much the choice of dependence alone can move the score.

In the classical case the answer is a beautiful object. Scatter points evenly over a sphere and look at their shadow on a flat plane. That shadow, called the disc copula, is known to be the best possible. For other powers, three statisticians proposed a family of shapes that bend the disc towards a diamond or a square, and conjectured from computer experiments that one particular member of the family is always the best.

This release proves that the conjecture holds only in the classical case and fails at every other power. In fact no member of the whole family is ever optimal, apart from the disc itself. The idea of the proof is easy to state. If a dependence pattern were optimal, shifting a little probability in a plus, minus, minus, plus pattern on a tiny rectangle could not help. That forces the density to be smooth in a particular sense, and the conjectured densities have a tiny kink where they cross the axes. An exact calculation in whole-number arithmetic also builds a concrete better pattern for one particular power.

Yet the family comes very close. Computations suggest that it falls short by only a few parts per million, so it remains an excellent practical approximation.

The limits matter. The true best pattern is still unknown, the size estimates are numerical rather than certified, and only two dimensions are treated.
