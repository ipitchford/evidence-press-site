How long must you watch an evolving system before you can work out how it started?

This mathematical paper studies that question using normal operators and continuous sensor observations. Its candidate result is striking: if stable reconstruction is possible over one finite interval beginning at time zero, then every shorter positive interval beginning there works too. Longer finite intervals also work.

There is an important catch. Stable does not mean equally reliable. As the observation window shrinks, the reconstruction can become much more sensitive to noise. One two-dimensional example in the paper shows exactly how quickly that sensitivity can grow.

Starting later is also different from stopping earlier. Under the theorem's assumptions, a delayed observation interval works precisely when the operator has a bounded inverse. If some modes fade arbitrarily quickly, losing the earliest observations can destroy the uniform recovery guarantee.

The proof turns an integrated bound on observation energy into analytic control at positive times. It then shows that states cannot become almost invisible on a short initial interval while remaining uniformly visible on a longer one.

Readers can inspect the complete proof, worked examples, and small verification scripts. Those scripts check examples and series coefficients, not the general theorem. This remains an unrefereed, AI-assisted candidate, and it does not supply a practical finite-sampling method or a universal noise tolerance.
