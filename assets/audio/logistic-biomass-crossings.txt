Migration can switch a population above and below its carrying capacity five times. An Evidence Press briefing. First, the background: picture a species living in a few habitat patches. Without movement, each patch settles at its carrying capacity, and the total is the sum of those capacities. Now let individuals move between the patches, with every migration rate scaled by one overall intensity. Depending on the patches, migration can leave the total population a little above that sum, meaning migration helps, or a little below it, meaning migration harms. In 2015, Roger Arditi, Claude Lobry and Tewfik Sari showed that with two patches the total can switch from one side to the other only once. In 2022, Bilel Elbetch and colleagues found three switches with three patches, and asked for a proof that three is the most. Now, the main finding: three is not the most. With three patches the exact maximum is five. An explicit example with five switches is checked in exact arithmetic, and six are proved impossible. For any number of patches, n, the number of switches is at most two to the power n, minus three, and at least n plus two can happen. With four patches, an exact example has six. As for how large the effect is: tiny. Between switches the total moves by at most a few parts per million, and the example needs finely tuned migration rates. So this is about what the model allows, not about real populations. As for how sure this is: every theorem has a written proof, and the examples are certified in exact arithmetic. Finally, the limits: review so far is internal and AI-assisted, and the literature search was bounded.
