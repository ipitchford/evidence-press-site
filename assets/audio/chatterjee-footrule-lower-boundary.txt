This briefing is about two ways of measuring how two quantities depend on each other.

Chatterjee's coefficient, called xi, asks how far one quantity is determined by the other. It is zero when they are independent, and one when the second is an exact function of the first, whatever the shape of that function. Spearman's footrule asks how close the two rankings are. It is one when the rankings agree, zero under independence, and it can fall to minus one half when the rankings are pushed as far apart as possible.

Which pairs of values can occur together? Marcus Rockel answered most of this question. One curved edge stayed open: when the rankings are pushed apart by a given amount, how small can the functional dependence be?

This paper closes that edge. For every negative footrule above the lowest value, it finds the least possible xi, shows that exactly one joint law of the ranks attains it, and gives a certificate that nothing does better. The extremal law has a simple recipe. The response takes one of two levels, depending on whether the predictor's rank lies below a threshold, and that threshold solves a linear integral equation with a triangular kernel.

One consequence is a sharp inequality. For any pair of continuous quantities, xi plus twice the footrule never falls below about minus zero point six zero four, and that limit cannot be improved. The paper also settles the regions under positive and negative quadrant dependence, and gives a partial answer for stochastically decreasing data, where the last edge is stated as a conjecture rather than a theorem.

There are limits. The results concern population coefficients, not estimates from samples. The closed form covers only the more strongly negative footrules; elsewhere the boundary is a finite numerical calculation, cross-checked but not certified. Rockel's earlier examples were already close, so the advance is exactness, uniqueness and proof.
