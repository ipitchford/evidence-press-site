Imagine walking around a ring of locations, with occasional shortcuts joining distant points. A short route across the network does not automatically mean a random walker quickly forgets where it started. This candidate asks what changes when movement around the ring has a fixed directional bias. The written proof gives two answers for an explicitly defined random network. With the larger shortcut density, the claimed mixing time grows only logarithmically with the number of locations. With fewer shortcuts, it grows on a square-root scale, up to logarithmic factors. The sparse upper and lower bounds do not match exactly. The argument covers four ways of measuring time, including a discrete walk that never deliberately stays still. Several obstacles matter. Fast mixing among shortcut endpoints can hide slow movement between them. Travel duration and destination are dependent. An average starting point is not the worst starting point. And a nonlazy walk can retain an even-odd obstruction. The proof treats each issue separately using random spacing, joint clock estimates and parity control. Readers can inspect the detailed proof modules and rerun exact rational checks of finite matrix identities. Those checks do not certify the asymptotic theorem. The weights and directional bias are fixed as the network grows; arbitrary weight rules and vanishing bias are excluded.
