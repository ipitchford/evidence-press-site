When a neuron label is not enough. An Evidence Press briefing for the eighteenth of October, twenty twenty-six.

Giving a neuron a meaningful label can help us understand a model. But what does that label actually let us predict?

This study examines one published transformer trained to solve small logical problems. Some units were labelled by the assignments that make a formula true. Those labels rank examples remarkably well. Yet turning an activation into a simple on or off answer can lose important information.

Consider one unit on a fresh bank of formulas. Its ranking score is about ninety-nine point two per cent. At the existing threshold, however, it detects only eighty-eight of one hundred and twenty-five positive examples. Good ranking and reliable threshold decisions are not the same thing.

The study also separates agreement with the model's actual answer from correctness about the underlying logic. And an exact example shows why knowing a formula's current truth table need not tell us what happens when a clause is replaced. The missing information concerns the clauses, not the supplied edit itself.

The fresh checks use the same checkpoint, not independently trained models. They do not settle broad debates about how neural representations work. The public package records the replayable checks and the limits on redistributing historical supporting material.
