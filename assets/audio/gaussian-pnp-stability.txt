Can a popular kind of image denoiser make an image-reconstruction method diverge? An Evidence Press briefing. First, the background. Many methods that rebuild images from blurred or incomplete data alternate two steps: one that fits the measurements, and one that cleans up noise. A common clean-up step averages each pixel with similar ones, using Gaussian weights, as in non-local means. In 2025, Sinha and colleagues noted that these methods seem to converge in practice, but that nobody had a proof or a counterexample. Now, the main finding. The answer depends on size. With the standard assumptions, the iterations are always stable for problems of up to three points, at every allowed step size. At four points they can fail: the paper gives explicit Gaussian weights and a fixed positive blur for which both standard iterations slowly diverge, and maps exactly where this happens. A twelve-point example shows the same thing for a convolution blur, though only for very small steps. Without equal self-weights, failure starts at three points. How big is the effect? Tiny. Errors grow by at most about seventeen parts in a million per iteration, so it would take hundreds of thousands of iterations to grow tenfold, while real runs use a few hundred. So the result shows that no blanket guarantee exists, not that real imaging pipelines are failing. The practical advice: choose step sizes in proportion to how well the denoiser mixes, or use a symmetrised denoiser, which the paper proves is always safe under a mild condition. Finally, the status.
