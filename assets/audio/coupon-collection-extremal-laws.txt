Imagine collecting a full set of stickers when every packet holds a group of them, and whoever fills the packets decides how the groups overlap. How quickly can a full set be finished, and how slowly can it be made while staying fair, with every sticker equally common? The same question arises when DNA reads arrive in batches, when computers contact random groups of servers, and when copies of data are spread across machines.

Earlier work conjectured that drawing uniformly from all possible groups is the slowest fair rule. That conjecture was disproved with a small geometry called the Fano plane, which left open how slow a fair rule can really be. On the fast side, it was conjectured that splitting the stickers into equal separate groups is always quickest.

This release answers several of these questions. When the stickers left out of each packet can form a special pattern called a Steiner design, sampling that pattern is the slowest fair rule after any number of packets, and only the design does this. For collections of every size, the slowest fair rule is also found in a family of critical cases, up to an error that fades as collections grow. And no fair rule is ever much slower than uniform sampling. When each packet holds at least half the stickers, the extra wait is less than one packet on average, and as packets come to hold almost everything it approaches a little over a third of a packet, a limit that is actually reached.

An exact computer search of every small case shows that the Fano plane is the slowest fair rule in its own example, and that six stickers in packets of three is the smallest case where uniform sampling is not the slowest. On the fast side, the release proves that equal separate groups are the unique fastest rule whenever the packet size divides the number of stickers, which settles that conjecture.

The idea is simple. Every fair rule leaves the same expected number of stickers missing; slow rules make them turn up one at a time, and fast rules make them vanish in clumps.

There are limits. The critical-case results describe large collections and settle in slowly, the exact slowest rule without a design is known only for small collections, and the fastest rule remains open in the remaining cases. Earlier work already reached the slowest value in some critical cases and supplied one key inequality, and the paper credits both. This is an unrefereed candidate, checked by internal AI-assisted review and exact computer checks rather than by external experts.
