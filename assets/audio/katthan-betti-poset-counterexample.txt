Same Betti poset. Different Stanley dimension. An Evidence Press briefing.

A useful mathematical summary keeps the information needed to answer a question. But can one summary serve two different theories? This candidate finds a precise limit.

The objects are monomial ideals, collections built from products of variables. Their least-common-multiple lattice records how the generators fit together. A smaller structure, called the Betti poset, retains the information supporting an algebraic resolution. The conjecture was that it also determines the ideal's Stanley projective dimension, which comes from decomposing monomials into simple pieces.

The example gives two ideals with six generators each. Their Betti posets are the same over every field. Yet their Stanley projective dimensions are three and two. One lattice has sixty elements; the other has fifty-nine. Removing a single element, invisible to the Betti poset, changes the decomposition invariant.

Explicit decompositions provide positive witnesses. A nine-point obstruction supplies the negative half, with a repaired argument that handles intervals which might coincide. The complete census of seven thousand, four hundred and forty-three five-atom lattices shows that six generators is the smallest possible threshold.

A squarefree version puts the same separation in one fourteen-variable ring. But important questions survive. The quotient assertion remains open, and neither original ideal violates Stanley's inequality. This is not a solution to that larger problem.
