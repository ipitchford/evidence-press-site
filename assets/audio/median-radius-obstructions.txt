Suppose you are looking for a central point in a network: a vertex whose greatest distance to any other vertex is as small as possible. A natural approach is to look nearby for a better choice. How far must you look before the absence of a strict improvement tells you that you have found a center? This candidate studies median graphs, a structured family that includes rectangular grids. It shows that cube dimension alone does not provide the proposed linear search radius. An explicit family requires a move whose length grows like a Fibonacci number before eccentricity strictly decreases. The construction connects two mathematical objects: integer solutions of separation inequalities, and distances from selected corners of a grid. Adding paths at those corners converts the example into ordinary, unweighted graph eccentricity. Combining this transfer with an established theorem of Alon and Vu gives a stronger, superexponential obstruction in every sufficiently large dimension. A complementary bound identifies the leading logarithmic scale for the restricted corner-profile model. The distinction is strict improvement. Equal-value moves can sometimes lead to a later decrease, and the result does not prove that every center-finding algorithm is slow.
