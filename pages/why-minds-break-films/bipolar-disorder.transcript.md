## Opening

Beth has experienced weeks of deep depression. At another time, she needed very little sleep, talked unusually fast and made plans that became risky. These were sustained changes from her usual self. Understanding that history matters when choosing treatment.

Bipolar disorder involves distinct episodes affecting mood, energy and activity. It is more than ordinary changes between feeling happy and sad. This episode explains contributing factors, what is known about the biology, and why treatment depends on the phase of illness.

This is general information for adults, not medical advice. Suspected mania or severe depression needs prompt professional assessment. Seek urgent local help if there is immediate danger or someone cannot stay safe. Do not stop or change prescribed treatment because of this video.

## Recognising distinct episodes

During mania, mood may be unusually elevated or irritable, with increased energy, less need for sleep and changes in judgement. Some people lose touch with reality. The disruption can be severe enough to need hospital care, even when the person feels exceptionally well.

Bipolar one includes at least one manic episode. Bipolar two involves depression and hypomania, a less severe form of elevated or irritable mood and increased activity, without full mania. Bipolar two can still cause serious suffering, especially through depression.

Assessment follows the person's history over time, including sleep, medicines, substances and physical health. Family observations can help, with the person's agreement. A single low mood, brief burst of energy or laboratory result does not settle the diagnosis.

## What contributes

Inherited vulnerability is strongly established. Many genetic differences contribute, usually with small individual effects. Some overlap with other mental health conditions. These findings do not identify one bipolar gene, and family history does not make the illness inevitable.

Adversity, stress and substance use can matter, but a risk factor is not the same as an episode trigger. Cannabis is associated with later bipolar symptoms; shared vulnerabilities complicate the causal picture. One event rarely provides a complete explanation for a person's illness.

Sleep has a particularly close relationship with episodes. Sleep loss can contribute to an episode, be an early sign of one, or worsen it. Mania can itself reduce the felt need for sleep. The relationship can therefore run in both directions.

## What changes in the brain

Researchers study brain networks involved in emotion, reward, attention and control. Chemical messengers, including dopamine, help those networks communicate. Their involvement does not establish a simple rule that mania means too much of one chemical and depression too little.

Other findings concern how cells regulate activity, supply energy and respond to stress. Inflammation may be relevant in some people. These are promising research directions, but differences measured during illness may reflect causes, consequences, treatment or other health conditions.

There is no complete biological explanation for why a particular episode begins or ends. Nor does bipolar disorder inevitably cause progressive loss of thinking ability. Treatment can help without a complete theory: responding to lithium does not show that someone lacked lithium.

## Treat the current episode

The first treatment question is: what needs treating now? Mania, bipolar depression and prevention of future episodes require different choices. Medicines sometimes called mood stabilisers are not interchangeable. Previous benefits, side effects, other illnesses and the person's preferences all matter.

For mania, effective options include certain antipsychotic medicines and lithium. Antipsychotic is the name of a medicine group; their use does not mean every person has psychosis. Severe episodes may need a combination and close support or hospital treatment.

Bipolar depression needs treatment with evidence for that phase. Quetiapine is one established option, with other choices depending on circumstances. Antidepressants alone are not recommended for bipolar one depression. Selected use alongside appropriate bipolar treatment requires clinical judgement and monitoring.

For a severe or resistant episode, a specialist may recommend ECT. During a brief general anaesthetic, a controlled electrical stimulus produces a seizure. It can be effective, but needs discussion of memory problems, including gaps that can persist.

## Prevent recurrence and monitor safety

After improvement, continuing treatment can reduce the chance of another episode. Lithium is a leading option, particularly for preventing mania, and several alternatives also work. The best plan takes account of previous response and whether depressive or manic episodes have been the main difficulty.

Monitoring is part of treatment. Lithium needs blood-level checks and monitoring of kidney and thyroid health. Dehydration and interacting medicines can increase toxicity risk. Other treatments may require checks for weight gain, sleepiness or other effects. Discuss pregnancy plans early with the care team.

## A plan for everyday life

Structured psychological care adds benefit alongside medication. It can teach early warning signs, coping with stress, steadier routines and better communication. Family sessions can help when wanted. The evidence mainly concerns this care added to medication, rather than replacing it.

Beth's plan includes regular sleep, people she can contact and an agreed response to early changes. Practical support with work, housing, physical health or substance use may also be needed. A plan made during a stable period can guide help when judgement becomes harder.

Recovery is more than a lower symptom score. It includes relationships, meaningful activity and treatment that remains tolerable. Episodes can recur, but recurrence is a reason to review care. With sustained support, many people build stable and satisfying lives.
