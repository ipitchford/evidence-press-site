## Opening

Sam wants to reduce his drug use. He has tried several times, but familiar places and difficult days bring strong urges. Missed appointments and strained relationships are adding to the pressure. He wants help that makes everyday life more manageable.

Drug use disorders involve harmful patterns of use and difficulty regulating them. Biology, learning and circumstances interact. Effective treatment depends on the drug involved, the person's health and what keeps the problem going.

This is general information for adults, not medical advice. Withdrawal can require medical care. Do not abruptly stop prescribed medicines because of a video. A qualified professional can help plan safer treatment.

## Different substances, different problems

Opioids, stimulants, cannabis and sedatives have different effects. Their risks and treatments are not interchangeable. Alcohol has its own episode in this series. Using several substances together makes assessment and treatment more complicated.

Physical dependence is a separate question. The body can adapt to a prescribed medicine, causing withdrawal if it is stopped. That can happen during appropriate treatment. Withdrawal or needing a different dose does not, by itself, establish addiction.

Assessment asks what is being used, how often, in what combinations, and with what consequences. It includes withdrawal, physical and mental health, other medicines and the person's circumstances. The aim is an honest picture and a useful plan.

## Why problems develop

Inherited differences can affect vulnerability through sensitivity to drugs, behaviour and mental health. Many genetic differences contribute small effects. Family history raises risk, but exposure and circumstances influence what happens. A person's future is not decided by their genes.

Availability, social networks, adversity and opportunities for a rewarding life also matter. Some people use drugs for pleasure, some for relief, and many for several reasons. Trauma is an important risk factor, but it does not explain every case.

## How the pattern persists

Drugs can strongly reinforce behaviour: their effects make repeating the action more likely. Places, people and feelings can become linked with those effects. Later, these cues may trigger wanting even when the experience brings less enjoyment than expected.

Repeated exposure can also change how brain cells communicate. Some changes contribute to tolerance, withdrawal or difficulty regulating responses. These changes differ between drugs and people. There is no single permanent dopamine level that explains every addiction.

Brain changes can make stopping difficult without making recovery impossible. New learning, treatment and changed circumstances can support recovery. Persistent memories or urges do not mean that someone will inevitably return to harmful use.

## Effective opioid treatment

For opioid use disorder, methadone and buprenorphine are the main established medicines. They can reduce withdrawal and craving, support continued treatment and reduce non-prescribed opioid use. Studies also find substantially lower death rates while people remain in treatment.

The clinician and patient choose together, considering safety, previous response and practical access. Treatment can continue for as long as it remains beneficial. Counselling and practical support should be offered, but unavailable counselling should not delay effective medication.

Detoxification alone is not sufficient continuing treatment for opioid use disorder. Tolerance falls after stopping. Returning to a previously used amount can then cause an overdose. A planned transition into ongoing care is important.

Naloxone can temporarily reverse an opioid overdose. People at risk, and those around them, can ask local services about access and training. Suspected overdose requires emergency help even if naloxone has been given. Protection and treatment belong together.

## Treatment for other drugs

For cocaine and other stimulant disorders, the strongest behavioural evidence is for contingency management. This means prompt, tangible rewards for agreed, verified progress, such as attending treatment or drug-negative tests. It usually sits alongside practical psychological support.

Talking treatment can help people recognise triggers, manage urges and build rewarding alternatives. Some medicines show benefits for selected stimulant problems, but their effects are generally more modest and uncertain. There is no universally agreed first-choice medicine for these disorders.

For cannabis use disorder, treatment commonly combines help with motivation and cognitive behavioural therapy: practical work on thoughts, habits and coping. The evidence is less certain than for opioid medicines. No medication has an established first-line role for cannabis use disorder.

Dependence on certain sedatives, including some medicines for anxiety and sleep, may need a gradual, individually planned reduction. Abrupt withdrawal can cause seizures or dangerous confusion. Withdrawal from GHB can also deteriorate quickly and needs specialist assessment.

## Continuing care

Continuing care also addresses housing, relationships, work, infection prevention and other health conditions. These are part of making treatment workable. Residential care may help some people, but the treatment delivered and follow-up afterwards matter more than the setting alone.

If use continues or returns, review the plan, safety and support. Automatic discharge can remove care when it is needed. Progress may include fewer harms, better health, staying engaged and greater stability, alongside reduced use or abstinence.

For Sam, recovery means more than getting through a few drug-free days. It means rebuilding a life he can sustain. The most useful plan combines treatment supported by evidence with practical opportunities, continuing support and room to adjust.
