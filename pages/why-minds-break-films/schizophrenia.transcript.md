## Opening

Jonah has been hearing a voice that other people cannot hear. Ordinary events feel unusually threatening, and daily tasks have become harder. He needs someone to take his experience seriously and help him feel safer. Assessment is the beginning of that help.

Schizophrenia can affect perception, beliefs, thinking, motivation and everyday functioning. It is not a split personality. Experiences vary greatly, and the diagnosis does not define the whole person. Understanding both symptoms and practical needs helps us build a useful treatment plan.

This is general information for adults, not medical advice. New or worsening psychotic experiences need prompt professional assessment. If someone is in immediate danger or cannot stay safe, seek urgent local help. Do not stop or change prescribed medication because of a video.

## Understanding the experience

Psychosis can include hearing voices, strongly held beliefs that others do not share, or difficulty organising thought. It can occur in several conditions, not only schizophrenia. Clinicians consider mood episodes, substances, medicines and physical or neurological illness, alongside the pattern over time.

Some people also struggle to get started, express emotion, speak easily or concentrate. These difficulties are not simply laziness. Depression, continuing psychosis and medication effects can add to them. A good assessment looks for contributors that can be treated.

## Why it can develop

Inherited vulnerability is substantial. Many common genetic differences each have small effects; some rare changes have larger effects. There is no single schizophrenia gene. Even people with very similar genetic inheritance can have different outcomes, so vulnerability is not destiny.

Development matters, often before the first psychotic episode, but relevant brain processes continue into adulthood. Early vulnerability does not mean inevitable illness or deterioration. Researchers are still tracing how genetic differences affect brain cells, their connections and later experience.

Adversity, social exclusion and some pregnancy or birth-related circumstances are associated with greater risk. Frequent use of strong cannabis probably contributes to psychosis in some people. Shared vulnerabilities also explain part of these links. No single exposure accounts for everyone's schizophrenia.

## What researchers know about the brain

One of the strongest biological findings concerns dopamine, a chemical messenger. In many people with psychosis, parts of the brain produce and release more of it. This finding concerns particular systems, not too much dopamine everywhere or in every person.

One proposed explanation is that altered signalling makes ordinary events feel unusually significant. That may help explain how threatening interpretations develop. It remains a model, not a reading of someone's thoughts. Dopamine alone does not explain all difficulties with motivation or thinking.

Other research examines how brain cells connect, coordinate activity and adjust their connections during development. Immune processes may also contribute. The idea that excessive removal of connections explains all schizophrenia is not established, and a routine scan cannot reveal one person's complete cause.

## Treat psychosis and prevent relapse

Antipsychotic medicines are the best-established treatment for hallucinations, delusions and disorganised thinking. They can also reduce relapse when effective treatment continues. There is no universally best first medicine. Choice should consider likely benefits, previous response, side effects and the person's preferences.

Some people prefer tablets. Others find a long-acting injection more convenient or easier to sustain. Both need a jointly agreed plan. Follow-up should ask about sleepiness, restlessness, movement problems, weight gain and other effects, as well as whether symptoms are improving.

If psychotic symptoms persist after two adequate medication trials, the team should assess whether clozapine is appropriate. First check that the earlier treatments had a fair trial and consider other reasons for poor response. Repeatedly switching standard medicines can delay useful care.

Clozapine requires careful monitoring, including blood tests and attention to heart problems, severe constipation and metabolic health. Its recommended role is established, while estimates of its advantage over other newer medicines remain uncertain in some comparisons. Benefits and burdens both need discussion.

## Support recovery in daily life

Psychological treatment can help people cope with experiences and reduce distress. In cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis, the person explores interpretations and tries practical coping strategies with a therapist. Average benefits are modest, and most evidence concerns therapy added to usual treatment.

Structured family support can explain experiences, improve communication and help people plan for difficulties. It can reduce the burden on relatives and may reduce relapse. This is support for a shared challenge; it does not mean the family caused the illness.

Early intervention teams can coordinate medication, therapy, family work and help with education or employment. Supported employment helps people who want a job seek ordinary paid work with continuing support. Training in attention and memory can also help, especially when linked to everyday goals.

## Review the whole plan

Physical health belongs in the plan. That includes checking weight, blood pressure and blood sugar, addressing smoking and making activity and ordinary health care accessible. Persistent low motivation deserves its own review; simply increasing medication does not reliably solve every difficulty.

Medication decisions should balance relapse protection with treatment burden. Some people may discuss carefully supported reduction with their clinician, but trials do not support routine early stopping. Any change needs an agreed plan, gradual adjustment when appropriate and monitoring for returning symptoms.

For Jonah, progress may mean feeling less threatened, reconnecting with people and returning to activities he values. Recovery differs between people and can include continuing support. The aim is a safer, more satisfying life, shaped with the person rather than defined by the diagnosis.
