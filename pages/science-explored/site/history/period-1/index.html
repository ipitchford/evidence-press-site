What survives of a technology?

A stone flake, a charred seed and a surviving length of cord answer different questions about human skill. Begin with the Smithsonian’s comparison of stone, bone and plant remains.

In the book’s Contents, open “Before 3000 BCE · Making and remembering”.

Tools and diet

Compare marks on bone with experimental examples. Matching marks can support an inference about use even when the implement itself is missing.

Stone tools and the archaeological record · Smithsonian Human Origins Program

Cultivation in several places

Put Kuk Swamp beside the Andes. Ask what a drainage feature, a starch grain and the remains of a meal can each establish about tending a plant.

The Neolithic Revolution · OpenStax, Rice University

Voyages without boats

The occupation of Sahul requires a sea crossing. Modern Pacific wayfinding helps explain the knowledge a crossing can require. It cannot identify the route taken by the earliest travellers.

Hawaiian wayfinding aboard Hōkūleʻa · Smithsonian Center for Folklife and Cultural Heritage

Fibre as engineering

Compare the surviving cord from Abri du Maras with a living tradition of bridge renewal in the Andes. Use the bridge to ask about fibres, inspection and collective labour. The modern practice cannot establish what the prehistoric cord was for.

Q’eswachaka and the living practice of Inka engineering · National Museum of the American Indian

Objects transformed by heat

What can a fired object reveal about the maker’s control over heat? Compare an early ceramic object with the much later evidence for Greek pots. Separate what can be inferred from the fired body from what depends on evidence for the workshop.

Making Greek vases · Smarthistory
