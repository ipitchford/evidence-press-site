MIT HST.512 Genomic Medicine, Spring 2004

  1. Lecture 1: Genomic Introduction

    The future is now; Genomic vs genetic; Heredity; Resequencing of the diagnostic process; Accelerating consumer activation.

  2. Lecture 2: Introduction to Biology and Genomic Measurement

    Basic Biology; Chromosomes; What does a gene look like?; Area between genes; Genome size; Genomes of other organisms; mRNA is made from DNA; Digitizing amino acid codes; Genetic Code; Molecular Biology; Protein targeting; Transcriptional Regulation; mRNA.

  3. Lecture 3: Measurement Techniques

    Discussion of paper: Coordinated reduction of genes of oxidative metabolism in humans with insulin resistance and diabetes: Potential role of PGC1 and NRF1.

  4. Lecture 4: Microarray - Massively Parallel Measurement

    What are the characteristics of a microarray? The first is that it should have a small form factor. One part of it’s – capable of measuring a significant fraction of some “-ome,” the genome, the transcriptome, or the proteome.

  5. Lecture 5: Limits of Technologies

    Limitations of massively parallel technologies.

  6. Lecture 6: Information Science at the Center of Genomic Medicine

    I’ll show you how, if you actually do the computation right, you can actually extract useful biomedical information out of it.

  7. Lecture 7: Informational Resources

    I’m going to talk to you today about the most important resources for finding and using biomedical information, especially information connected with the study of the human genome.

  8. Lecture 8: Complex Traits: What to Believe

    The current efforts in human genetics to understand the role that inherited DNA sequence variation plays in regulating what are called complex traits.

  9. Lecture 9: Machine-learning Approach

    Today I’m going to talk to you about basic genetics, what geneticists do, and how genetics is moving into the genomic area by increasing the size, the scope, and the quality of genetic studies.

  10. Lecture 10: Association with Markers

    I’m going to talk about how to analyze BLAST data. And the principle is that there is no such a thing as putting your data into a machine and expecting to get an answer. The type of analysis you make is always related to the question you’re asking.

  11. Lecture 11: The Importance of Data Representation

    We’ll go through two very prototypical study designs, one is a two-way comparison. The second one is the time series, or a dosage study. Finally, we get to the topic of data representation. We talk about transforming data to uncover internal structures.

  12. Lecture 12: Pharmacogenomics

    This is how I view pharmacogenomics. It’s the use of genetic information to determine who will respond favorably or unfavorably.

  13. Lecture 13: Case Hx: Complex Traits

    We’re going to begin by of getting at this question of why complex trait human genetics is so difficult. And then go through each of the steps that you would do if you were actually doing this work.

  14. Lecture 15: Microarray Disease Classification

    This is the Use of Microarrays for Disease Classification.

  15. Lecture 16: Microarray Disease Classification II

    I thought what we would do today is go through an example. And the example is related to breast cancer and the paper from Nature 2002 that we talked about.

  16. Lecture 17: Direct Prediction of Outcome / Mortality

    Today I’ll just talk a little bit more generally about a few observations that I’ve had, about reliability microarray studies, about classification problem in general, more about phenotypes, and then review some literature that are well cited.

  17. Lecture 18: Case Hx: Cancer Diagnostics

    I’m going to start by giving examples of a couple of patients that I saw in the Jimmy Fund clinic at the Dana-Farber that were typical.

  18. Lecture 19: Modeling and Reverse Engineering

    Modeling and what modeling can achieve, or why people are doing it at all, or is it doable at all. Reverse engineering, where you really want to reconstruct how the system looks like– what you are going to use for modeling.

  19. Lecture 20: Practical Genomic Medicine

    So what we’ll talk about today is a very practical genomic medicine, by which I mean not only stuff that we think is going to be imminent in two years but what it means today to do genomic medicine, nothing future, just today.
