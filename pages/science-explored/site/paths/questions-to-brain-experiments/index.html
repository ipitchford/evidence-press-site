Learning path

From a brain question to an experiment

Learners with introductory psychology who want to understand the logic behind cognitive-neuroscience studies.

Follow the Human Brain course from its central questions through methods, experimental design and competing interpretations. The route preserves the relevant lecture order and adds a continuing task: refine one question into two discriminating experiments.

  1. · Lecture 1: Introduction

    Begin with the course’s aims so that the methods are attached to questions rather than learned as a list of technologies.

    Write one question about cognition that you would like evidence to answer; distinguish it from a general topic such as “vision”.

  2. · Lecture 4: Cognitive Neuroscience Methods I

    Now compare the range of methods the course introduces for investigating those questions.

    Make a four-column table: question, measured quantity, comparison and remaining uncertainty. Add two methods from the lecture.

  3. · Lecture 5: Cognitive Neuroscience Methods II

    Complete the methods pair before choosing an approach for your own question.

    Add another approach and describe what complementary observation it could provide. Avoid claiming that one method settles every part of the question.

  4. · Lecture 6: Experimental Design

    Move from recognising methods to constructing an experimental comparison, using the course’s face-recognition context and design brief.

    Read the linked experimental-design assignment. Sketch two linked experiments: the second should challenge an explanation still compatible with the first.

  5. · Lecture 7: Category Selectivity, Controversies, and MVPA

    Use a domain with explicit competing interpretations to test whether your proposed comparisons could distinguish accounts.

    For two possible accounts, state a different prediction. Identify which observation would leave the accounts unresolved.

  6. · Lecture 21: Brain Networks

    Finish by extending the question from local organisation to relationships between regions.

    Rewrite one network claim to specify the evidence it would need. Identify an anatomical, statistical or task-related ambiguity you would investigate next.
