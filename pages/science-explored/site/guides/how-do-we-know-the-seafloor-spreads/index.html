Topic guide

How do we know the seafloor spreads?

Use the magnetic stripes on either side of an ocean ridge to predict where crust formed. Then decide what evidence a geologist could have asked for in 1963.

Start with Seafloor spreading with magnetic stripes and reversal scale

In Tanya Atwater’s animation, use the reversal scale to identify the most recently formed crust. Pause after the next colour change. Trace both edges of the new band away from the ridge, then follow its offset across a transform fault.

Compare a second model

Open Magnetic reversal stripes, a silent Open University animation. Follow one band of rock from the ridge to the edge of the screen. Its magnetic direction stays fixed as its distance from the ridge grows.

Predict the other half

Draw a ridge down the middle of a sheet. Give the magnetic field three successive intervals of unequal length. Add new crust to both sides at a constant rate. Cover one side and use the other to predict the order and widths of its bands. Which prediction would survive a change in the spreading rate on one side?

From a pattern to a test

The animation starts with spreading as an assumption. Work backwards from the observations in the preceding geology lesson. Identify one measurement that could distinguish a reversal in magnetisation from a change in the kind of rock beneath the ship.

A decision in 1963

Vine and Matthews could reproduce the broad shape of magnetic anomalies over a ridge. One local fit was poor. Would another survey along the same track tell you as much as an independently dated rock sample? Ian Pitchford’s A Connected History of Science and Technology asks which test a geologist should fund next. In Historical judgement, find “Would you fund the next seafloor test?”

Follow the ocean-ridge learning path

See NOAA’s reconstruction of plate movement. Compare the age pattern in the ocean basins with the crust forming at a single ridge.
