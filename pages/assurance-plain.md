# Who Checks the Machines?

*Governments are about to be able to read everything. Whether they can trust what they read is a different problem, with different economics. It is the problem that binds.*

In 2025 a British government tool called [Consult](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/ground-breaking-use-of-ai-saves-taxpayers-money-and-delivers-greater-government-efficiency) read more than 50,000 responses to a public consultation and sorted them into themes. The machine time cost £240, about the price of an office chair. Two practised panels of human experts then checked its work. Their 22 hours cost, at ordinary loaded rates of £50 to £80 an hour, between £1,100 and £1,760 — five to seven times the machine bill. In the first government AI deployment measured end to end, checking the answers accounted for 82 to 88 per cent of the direct cost of the exercise.

That ratio, and what it would take to change it, is the subject of this essay.

The trend everyone quotes runs the other way. At fixed capability, the price of a unit of AI agent work (a model reading, reasoning and writing on its own for an extended stretch) has been falling at a median of roughly fifty-fold per year on [Epoch AI's published series](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends). The length of task that leading models can finish has been doubling every three to four months on the fits published by [the evaluation group METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-1-29-time-horizon-1-1/). Put the two together and a question follows naturally: what could a department do with a million agent sessions? Read every consultation response, every local plan, every hospital account, every published study bearing on a question, one machine pass per document, overnight?

It could, and soon. But the question skips the part that binds. Generating candidate analysis is the cheap half of evidence. The expensive half is establishing that what was generated is true, properly sourced and reproducible, and that half is not getting fifty times cheaper each year on its own. As generation cheapens, the checking share of every analytical pound rises mechanically. The reading-everything future is real and it is buyable, but only by whoever builds the verification machinery first.

## A session is not a unit of insight

Public discussion of government AI tends to assume a simple production function: more agent sessions in, more policy insight out. Four pieces of arithmetic, each with recent empirical backing, say the function does not exist. What a session contributes depends on the machinery around it, and mostly on the machinery that does the checking.

**Copies are not opinions.** When many agents answer the same question and their errors are even slightly correlated (shared training data, shared architectures, shared blind spots), averaging their answers saturates fast. Survey statisticians have known the algebra for decades: at a pairwise error correlation of just 1 per cent, a million calls carry the evidential weight of about one hundred independent opinions, however many more you buy. At 10 per cent, ten. And the evidence points to the pessimistic end. [One study](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02646v1) found that thirty agents debating a hard benchmark produced no more diversity of answers than a single agent working alone. [Another](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04313), comparing hundreds of models, found their mistakes becoming *more* alike as they improve, so a second opinion from a rival company's model increasingly means paying twice for the same opinion. A [third](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-026-01268-y), in *Nature Machine Intelligence*, found no sign that mixing model families escapes the ceiling.

The escape is architectural, and it matters more than the ceiling itself. The saturation applies to redundant answers to the *same* question. If a million episodes each tackle a different sub-question (a different document, a different data pull), the algebra never bites, because the episodes are no longer estimates of one common quantity. Architecture decides whether a deployment is a very expensive poll of one newspaper or a census of a library.

**Queues do not parallelise.** If 5 per cent of a job must be done in strict sequence, no army of agents delivers more than a twentyfold speed-up; if 30 per cent, barely threefold. Amdahl's law, from 1960s computing, and it applies with full force to agent teams. When Anthropic set [sixteen parallel agents to build a C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler), progress stalled the moment they reached the Linux kernel: every agent hit the same bug, fixed it, and overwrote the others' fixes. Sixteen agents were no better than one, because all sixteen were queuing at the same serial obstacle. Progress resumed only when the human operator restructured the task into genuinely parallel pieces. Questions of the form "what does this one causal chain imply" have long queues and low ceilings. Questions of the form "what do all 50,000 responses say" have almost none.

**You cannot extract from sources more than they contain.** No amount of processing adds information about the world that the inspected documents lack; a million readings of the same 200 papers buy little beyond a few dozen well-diversified ones. Mass agent work earns its keep only when the *corpus* is vast relative to human reading bandwidth. Call it the evidence census: one pass, or a small tree of passes, per source object, over tens of thousands to millions of objects. The Consult deployment is the prototype at 50,000 objects. Outside the census class, useful scale tops out at numbers closer to the tens of agents the research literature actually studies.

**Long chains rot.** A task of one hundred steps, each 99 per cent reliable given the steps before it, succeeds 37 per cent of the time end to end. Raise per-step reliability to 99.9 per cent and the chain succeeds 90 per cent of the time. Everything that buys that extra nine is a verification mechanism: tests, retries, checkpoints, a known-good reference to compare against. The compiler project's author reported that the task verifier had to be nearly perfect or the agents would diligently solve the wrong problem, and that most of the human effort went into tests and build infrastructure rather than into the agents. Long-horizon autonomy is a verification problem wearing a capability costume.

Notice what all four bounds have in common. None says agents are weak. Each says that the value of agent work is set by the machinery around it, and that the load-bearing part of the machinery is the part that checks.

## The one deployment measured end to end

Consult supplies the only full accounting so far, and it is worth reading closely. Twenty-two expert hours spread over 50,000 responses is 1.58 seconds per response: necessarily a sampled audit of aggregates, never an item-by-item review. Nor did the machine scrape through on a technicality. Its categorisations agreed with one or both expert panels almost 83 per cent of the time, while the two panels agreed with *each other* only 55 per cent of the time — an easier test than pairwise agreement, so it shows the tool is usable rather than superior, but usable it was.

Sampling theory explains why the audit could be so small, and where that stops working. For large collections of similar outputs, the statisticians' "rule of three" says that certifying an error rate below 1 per cent, with 95 per cent confidence, needs about 300 randomly sampled clean checks, *whatever the size of the collection*. Three hundred checks certify a corpus of 50,000 items or five million equally well. The cost of assurance scales with the error bound you demand, not with the volume of machine output.

Three conditions attach, and each becomes a research problem later in this essay. The sample must be genuinely random. The checker must actually catch the defects they inspect (real reviewers miss things, and nobody has yet measured the miss rate for this kind of work). And errors must not hide in corners a random sample rarely reaches, which is exactly where an adversary will put them. An average error rate is not always the right target either: one fabricated quotation can matter more than a thousand harmless misclassifications. But the basic arithmetic is friendlier than intuition suggests, and it is the reason the reading-everything future is auditable at all.

## What machines can check, and what they cannot

Not all claims are equally checkable, and the differences are where the money is. Picture the contents of an analytical report as a stack of four layers. At the bottom sit extractable facts and citations: a quotation either appears in the frozen source document or it does not, and a machine can check every single one at near-zero marginal cost, provided the source has been "frozen" — fingerprinted cryptographically so that it cannot quietly change after the fact. Above that sit statistical claims, which can be re-computed: given the data and the code, a machine re-runs the calculation and confirms the published number, and even without the data it can check a document's internal arithmetic. (The statcheck programme found that [roughly half of published psychology papers](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-015-0664-2) contain at least one internal reporting inconsistency, using nothing but the papers' own text.) Above that sit causal claims, where the formal logic can sometimes be certified but the substantive assumptions cannot. And at the top sit framing and value judgements, which no machine can convert into a checkable claim, and should not pretend to.

The strategic move for any institution is to push its analytical products *down* this stack, with claims tagged by their type and their check, sources frozen and computations replayable, so that machines verify the bottom layers exhaustively and scarce human judgement concentrates at the top, where only humans can supply it.

One measured detail shows how much this matters in practice. Recent audits of AI "deep research" tools find that their citation *links* resolve 94 to 100 per cent of the time, while the cited sources actually *support* the claims made only 39 to 77 per cent of the time. The check everyone builds first (does the link work?) is the one with almost no discriminating power. The gap between "the source exists" and "the source says that" is where the checking effort belongs.

## The flood arrives whether or not the forecasts do

Everything so far assumes government chooses to use agents. The strongest reason to build checking machinery does not.

In 2017, the US Federal Communications Commission received more than 22 million public comments on its net-neutrality rule. The [New York Attorney General later established](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2021/attorney-general-james-issues-report-detailing-millions-fake-comments-revealing) that nearly 18 million of them were fabricated. A single 19-year-old, using automated software, submitted 9.3 million fake supporting comments; an industry campaign paid roughly $4.2 million for 8.5 million more — about 49 cents each, for text of template quality, years before modern language models existed. Text generation is now three to four orders of magnitude cheaper and individually styled. Whatever a department decides about running agents itself, the channels through which it *receives* evidence (consultations, calls for evidence, correspondence) face this at scale. That some synthetic evidence is already arriving in the UK is hard to doubt; the forecastable event is its detection and public documentation, which the [technical version of this argument](https://evidencepress.org/observatory/assurance/) assesses at a calibrated 0.65 by the end of 2029.

The tempting response is detection software, and it is a losing game that loses in the worst available way. Detectors are [theoretically bounded](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156) by the shrinking statistical distance between human and machine text, and their false positives [fall disproportionately on non-native English writers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819) and unusual styles. Under UK public law, consultation responses must be conscientiously considered; a screening regime that quietly discards genuine submissions because a classifier misfired, with errors that track protected characteristics, is an invitation to judicial review, not a safeguard.

The workable alternative builds provenance in rather than screening fakery out. Commit first, audit second: before any sample is drawn, the operator publishes a cryptographic fingerprint of the entire corpus and all outputs, and the sample indices come from a public randomness source nobody can steer. An audit of a collection the operator can quietly repair, once they learn which items will be inspected, certifies nothing. Offer a verified-identity tier alongside an open one, so authentication adds signal without excluding anyone. Require campaign organisers to disclose coordinated submissions, as the Elections Act 2022 already requires for digital political material. And beneath all of it, aggregate by argument rather than by volume: cluster submissions making the same case and cap any one cluster's weight, so that ten thousand copies of a talking point count as one argument, not ten thousand voices. (A cap is itself a judgement, since sheer mobilisation can be evidence of who cares and who is affected, so it must be declared rather than buried in the pipeline.) A consultation analysis built this way can publish, alongside its conclusions, the fraction of fabricated submissions it would take to flip each headline finding. Contamination-resistance becomes a stated property instead of an assumption.

This is the sense in which the recommendation is forecast-proof. If agent capability keeps compounding, government needs this machinery to use agents at scale. If capability stalls, it needs much of the same machinery to defend its evidence channels against everyone else's agents. The shared core appears in both columns: provenance receipts, frozen corpora, sampled audit.

## Building the checking machinery

Little of what is needed is new science. It is mature machinery from older assurance industries that has never been pointed at AI-generated evidence. Election administration spent two decades building [risk-limiting audits](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10035): sequential checks that certify an outcome with a guaranteed cap on the probability of wrongly certifying, examining as few items as the evidence permits and escalating automatically when it resists. [Prediction-powered inference](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adi6000), published in *Science* in 2023, combines machine labels on a whole corpus with human labels on a small random subsample to yield confidence intervals for the published figure ("62 per cent of respondents opposed") that stay valid whatever the machine's error pattern. His Majesty's Revenue and Customs runs a random enquiry programme alongside its targeted investigations, because targeted checks alone are gameable and cannot estimate the true error rate. Laboratory medicine routes blind test samples through accredited labs continuously, because the assurers must themselves be assured. Each of these transfers, with engineering rather than gambling.

The institutional design problem deserves equal billing with the statistics, because once an assurance stamp is scarce and valuable, the failure mode is rubber-stamping. The known countermeasures: a randomised audit floor that no risk model can game; planted defects in the live queue, so that the gap between announced and blind detection rates, the rubber-stamping rate itself, becomes directly observable; and a published receipt in place of a pass/fail stamp, stating what was checked, by which method, to what certified bound, and what was never covered at all.

The [full essay](https://evidencepress.org/observatory/assurance/) decomposes the programme into sixteen concrete projects, each sized for a small team with capable agents doing the mechanical work, each with a resolution criterion that can fail, and each scored for probability of delivery by three independent reviewer profiles. The whole portfolio costs about £1.26 million, less than a single mid-sized departmental evaluation, and almost every project fits inside a year:

| Tier | Delivery odds | Projects | Combined cost | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Near-certainties | 0.80–0.88 | 2 | ~£65k | Demonstrate that a sampled audit can be fooled by an operator who repairs items after learning the sample, then defeat the attack with published cryptographic commitments |
| Better than even | 0.55–0.68 | 5 | ~£320k | Valid confidence intervals for consultation statistics from machine labels plus a small human-checked sample |
| Coin flips | 0.40–0.52 | 5 | ~£460k | Measure how often different companies' models make the same mistakes on real policy tasks |
| Hard tail | 0.16–0.33 | 4 | ~£410k | Blind proficiency testing of the evidence assurers themselves: high payoff, but it needs a government host, which is why no researcher can deliver it alone |

The two near-certainties deliver in about four months for £65,000 combined. Adding the whole better-than-even tier brings the total under £400,000 and seven months, and would replace most of the argument's unmeasured quantities with numbers carrying intervals. The pattern in the ranking is instructive. What agents cannot compress is now the scarce input: expert adjudication, institutional hosting, contracted adversaries, and calendar time itself.

Behind the portfolio sits a theoretical gap, and a recent event shows both the gap and the way through it. On 1 August 2026, [OpenAI published ten results](https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/) in mathematics and theoretical computer science produced by an internal model, for roughly $2,000 of search compute. What matters here is not the mathematics but the trust arrangement. Nobody is asked to take the laboratory's word: each result ships as a formal proof checked by the small, mechanically independent kernel of the Lean proof assistant, and the human mathematicians' review concentrates on the one question machines cannot settle — whether the formal statements faithfully capture the informal problems. A powerful generator, a tiny trusted checker, human scrutiny at the semantic boundary. No equivalent yet exists for evidence: no calculus that says what a decision-maker may rely on when a conclusion has passed through many sources, models and transformations — under which assumptions, with what residual risk, until when. Building one is the deepest project in the programme, roughly £1.5 million across five strands, and the reviewers' scepticism is precisely located: not that the formal work would fail, but that the definitions might carve reality wrongly, a risk no agent compresses, because arguing about what the types should mean is human work.

## What this buys

It would be easy to read all of this as a brake. It is closer to a description of the accelerator's missing pedal linkage.

Built properly, census-class agent work changes what public evidence is. "We considered all responses" stops being a courtesy phrase and becomes a checkable claim with a certified error bound attached. Questions never asked because the reading was unaffordable (what do all the local plans, all the trust accounts, all the tribunal decisions actually say) become routine. On the government's own figures, consultation analysis alone consumes some 75,000 analyst-days a year, about £20 million in staffing; the same expertise, moved from transcription to judgement, is the prize. And the capability compounds: every class of claim that gains a mechanical check widens what can be safely delegated, which produces more verified output, which extends the machinery further. Agents will increasingly check agents, and the correlated-error results say that is safe exactly when the chain bottoms out in something mechanically independent: a frozen source, a re-run computation, a proof kernel. That is what this infrastructure supplies.

The binding constraint on AI in government, then, is not model capability, which no department controls and which is in any case racing ahead of the instruments built to measure it. It is the productivity of checking — and that, unlike capability, is something a modest budget and a determined institution can start moving this year. Whoever does gets to run the reading-everything future safely, years before anyone else. In a period when capability compounds, years are the currency that matters.
