Topic guide

What changes when decisions depend on other people?

Make a small strategic model explicit, then separate best responses, equilibrium and desirable outcomes.

For: Serious beginners in game theory and social decision-making.

A choice changes character when its consequences depend on someone else’s choice. You must consider what the other person can do, what they care about and what they know. Game theory provides a language for making those assumptions explicit; it does not supply those assumptions automatically.

The starting Yale lecture builds a game from a classroom example. Its written first-class summary, dated autumn 2007, makes a valuable distinction between outcomes and payoffs. The same visible outcome can matter differently to people with different preferences. That is why a table of rewards should be examined before declaring a particular action rational.

Follow the second lecture to add beliefs about the other player. Best-response examples then ask what a player should do given a specified expectation. Nash equilibrium adds mutual consistency: each choice is considered against the choices of the others. In the selected coordination lecture, equilibrium is explicitly separated from a good collective outcome. A stable pattern need not be a desirable one.

Gendler’s treatment of the prisoners’ dilemma and the commons supplies another disciplinary framing. It helps connect a formal structure to a collective-decision question, but an analogy still needs checking. Do the people have the proposed options? Are the payoffs plausible? Can they communicate, make commitments or expect to meet again? Those are changes to the model, not minor background details.

The final behavioural-games selection points towards a further task: comparing predicted choices with observed ones. A discrepancy can expose a poor behavioural assumption, an incorrect payoff model or an unrepresented feature of the setting. It should not automatically be called irrationality.

The exercise keeps the game small enough to inspect by hand. Find best replies, change one payoff and repeat. Then list what the table omits. This route is a preparation for modelling and criticism, not personal financial or negotiation advice. For a deeper sequence, follow the accompanying path into sequential choices, information sets and repeated interaction.

Change one payoff

Create a two-player, two-action table with explicit outcomes. Circle each player’s best reply to each possible action of the other. Change one payoff and repeat; explain what information the table leaves out.

Key terms

Payoff
How a model represents the value of an outcome to a player.
Best response
A choice that is best given specified beliefs or choices of others.
Nash equilibrium
A set of choices in which each is a best response to the others.
