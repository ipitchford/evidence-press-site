Topic guide

How do teaching goals, tasks and feedback fit together?

Design one lesson whose intended outcome, practice task and assessment call for the same kind of thinking.

For: Educators, tutors and learners designing a lesson or small course.

Imagine a lesson that asks learners to compare two explanations. The practice activity asks them to copy definitions, and the final test asks them to recall dates. Even if every component is competently delivered, the assessment cannot tell you whether the intended comparison was learned. This is the concrete design problem behind alignment.

Carnegie Mellon’s alignment guide connects three decisions: what the learner should be able to do, what activity prepares them to do it, and what assessment would reveal that ability. The starting NPTEL lecture names this relationship; the following implementation lecture is kept beside it so that the principle has a route into an actual design.

Begin with a verb and an object. “Understand memory” is difficult to inspect. “Compare two explanations for a result and identify an observation that distinguishes them” gives the learner a visible task. The assessment can then ask for the same kind of reasoning with a new example. This does not require every exercise to look exactly like the final assessment. It requires a defensible connection between the practice and the desired performance.

The learning-by-doing and feedback selections address what happens between the objective and the assessment. A learner needs an opportunity to attempt the relevant operation and find out where the attempt fell short. Scaffolding adds a further choice: which part of a demanding task should initially be supplied, demonstrated or simplified? That support can be reconsidered as the learner becomes more capable.

A digital tool is one possible way to implement a task. It should not decide the task’s purpose for you. The wider Instruction for Good Learning course explicitly discusses instructional situations and technology choices. Its theoretical commitments are attributed to that course; their presence in a catalogue is not a proof that one school of pedagogy settles every design question.

Use the exercise to repair a mismatch in a small lesson before redesigning a whole course. The result is a reviewable teaching plan, not evidence that learning has already improved. That further claim requires an appropriate assessment and observed learner performance.

Limits of this route

The plan can reveal a mismatch, but it cannot show that learners improved before outcomes are observed. Official course outlines establish selected topics; a course’s pedagogical commitments are not adopted as universal consensus. Source checks establish scope and selection usefulness; they do not claim full-video viewing, human review or scientific validation.
