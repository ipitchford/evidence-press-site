How do we recognise and retrieve words?

Ask what knowing a word involves and how recognition, retrieval and production tasks make different demands on that knowledge.

For: Learners moving from introductory linguistics to processing questions.

Knowing a word involves more than recognising that its sound or spelling looks familiar. You may know how it is used, what it can combine with and which meanings are plausible in a particular setting. The useful question is therefore not just where words are “stored”, but what knowledge a task requires and how that knowledge becomes available.

The opening pair of lectures explicitly links word meaning with lexical access. Use the first to establish the problem and the second to continue the same course’s treatment. The separate three-part word unit supplies another route, while the production and visual-recognition selections change the direction or modality of the task. A useful comparison holds the target word constant while asking what differs between hearing it, reading it and trying to say it.

The mental-lexicon chapter in Essentials of Linguistics distinguishes learned linguistic information from predictable patterns and includes morphemes and idioms in its account. This is more flexible than imagining an alphabetical dictionary inside the head. It also supplies a concrete puzzle: an expression’s familiar parts do not always predict its familiar meaning.

When comparing processing explanations, ask what the learner or participant actually does. Recognising a written item as a word, explaining its meaning and producing a related word are different tasks. A difference between them can suggest a hypothesis, but it does not alone locate a separate mental store. An explanation needs a prediction that distinguishes it from plausible alternatives.

The exercise starts with your linguistic knowledge rather than collecting response-time data. Separating form, meaning and use makes the model’s commitments visible. If you later try an experiment, specify the items, comparison and scoring before looking for a pattern. Word length, familiarity and language experience can otherwise become unexamined differences between examples.

The lecture selections were checked against institutional course outlines and their order, not through full viewing. The explanatory distinction comes from the retrieved reading; a particular computational or neural model is not endorsed. Sentence parsing is the next step, where knowledge of individual words must be connected to their arrangement.

Limits of this route

The textbook section supplies a conceptual account of learned linguistic information. It does not by itself identify a storage location or settle a processing model. NPTEL selection is based on ordered official outlines, not a review of experimental results within recordings. Source checks establish scope and selection usefulness; they do not claim full-video viewing, human review or scientific validation.
