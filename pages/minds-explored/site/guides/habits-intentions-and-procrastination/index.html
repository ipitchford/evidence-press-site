Topic guide

Why do intentions lose to habits or procrastination?

Compare philosophical, learning and time-preference accounts without turning an explanatory model into a moral diagnosis or a treatment claim.

For: Readers comparing philosophical and experimental accounts of self-regulation.

An intention can be sincere and still fail to organise the next action. That observation leaves several possible explanations: a cue may trigger a routine, an immediate reward may become more attractive, the task may be unclear, or the setting may make the intended action difficult. This guide compares explanatory models before turning any of them into a judgement about character.

Gendler’s 2011 lecture on weakness of will brings philosophical accounts, delay of gratification and discounting into the same discussion. It also presents proposed strategies for self-regulation. The date matters: a course discussion is not a current systematic review of those interventions, and a plausible strategy is not a guarantee that it will work for a particular person.

The two virtue-and-habit lectures clarify a different question. Aristotle’s account concerns what it is to acquire a virtue, not simply how frequently a response occurs. The continuation includes a challenge from situationist psychology. Compare this with Bouton’s account of learning, which distinguishes goal-directed action and habit within experimental learning theory. Similar everyday vocabulary can conceal different definitions and different evidence.

The behavioural-economics selections then make time explicit. A model of intertemporal choice asks how outcomes at different times enter a decision. A hyperbolic-discounting model is one specified proposal within that family. To understand it, draw the choices and dates before deciding whether it resembles a real case. Changing valuations, changing information and changing opportunities need not be the same process.

The exercise uses a fictional delayed task to separate a reward-value explanation from a cue-driven routine. Make the two predictions as different as possible. If both accounts can explain every imaginable result, the example has not yet become a useful test.

This route does not diagnose procrastination or turn a missed deadline into evidence of weak moral character. It provides concepts for comparing explanations, with explicit limits on the claims supported by older lectures. A deeper continuation is to follow the full intertemporal-choice sequence and compare its assumptions with a carefully specified everyday decision.

Key terms

Intertemporal choice
A choice involving outcomes at different times.
Discounting
Representing how an outcome’s value changes with its delay.
Habit
In learning research, a response pattern whose control is distinguished from current goal-directed evaluation.
