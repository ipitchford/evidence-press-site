Topic guide

What do conditioning experiments explain about learning?

Distinguish cue–outcome learning, action–outcome learning and broader uses of the word habit before judging what an experiment explains.

For: Readers beginning the experimental study of learning.

A signal can predict an event, and an action can change what happens next. Both can alter behaviour, but they pose different experimental questions. Calling every repeated behaviour a “habit” can hide that difference. This guide uses conditioning to practise identifying the relation that was learned, before asking how far the explanation reaches.

The starting MIT session includes classical and operant conditioning and a section on their limits. Yale’s discussion of Skinner supplies historical context. Read them alongside Mark Bouton’s Noba module, which distinguishes learning about relations between events from learning about the consequences of one’s own actions. An example is useful only if you can specify the cue, the response and the outcome. Merely noticing that something happened several times is not yet an account of what was learned.

The two NPTEL principles lectures let you keep those arrangements separate. Afterwards, the reinforcement-schedule lecture adds a new variable: an action need not be followed by an outcome on every occasion. Work through one change at a time. If you alter the cue, reward and setting together, it becomes much harder to identify which relation explains a change in behaviour.

There are also limits to an easy “association in, association out” picture. Bouton discusses goal-directed behaviour, observational learning and the return of a response after extinction. These distinctions are reasons to ask what the organism knows and what the current outcome is worth, rather than assuming that disappearance of a response means the earlier learning was erased.

The final selection deliberately changes discipline. Gendler compares habituation with Aristotle’s account of acquiring virtue. A comparison can suggest a question; it does not establish that a philosophical ideal and a laboratory procedure are the same thing. Write down where the analogy helps and where it needs an extra argument.

The exercise uses a harmless fictional routine. It is not an instruction to condition another person, train away distress or conduct an unsupervised clinical intervention. The useful skill is specifying a testable learning relation and noticing when the evidence supports a narrower conclusion than the everyday story.

Key terms

Classical conditioning
Learning a predictive relation between events.
Operant conditioning
Learning about consequences that depend on an action.
Extinction
Reduced responding after a previously predictive or reinforced relation changes.
