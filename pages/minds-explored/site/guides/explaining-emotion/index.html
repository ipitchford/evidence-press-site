What are competing explanations of emotion?

Distinguish feeling, bodily change, action and social function, then ask what evidence links them.

For: A reader who wants to compare explanations instead of learning a single list of emotions.

When someone says they are afraid, several things may be involved: an unpleasant feeling, a change in bodily activity, attention to danger and preparation to act. These are related observations, not four names for one measurement. The opening of MIT’s Emotion and Motivation lecture distinguishes pleasantness or unpleasantness from activation, and distinguishes an emotion from a longer-lasting mood. This gives us a vocabulary for comparing explanations.

One question concerns the relationship between bodily changes and conscious feeling. In the historical bodily-first account discussed in the MIT transcript, perceiving changes in the body contributes to the emotion. A different intuitive sequence puts the feeling first and the bodily response afterwards. Writing these sequences as arrows makes the disagreement clearer, but does not settle it. We would need evidence about timing and about what happens when one part of the sequence changes. The lecture’s historical demonstrations should not be treated as a current replication review.

A second question concerns function. What can an emotional response help an organism do? The Noba functions-of-emotions module organises its account across effects within a person, between people and within social life. An expression can influence another person as well as accompany a feeling. An explanation of that influence is different from a claim about what bodily event produces the feeling in the first place.

Evolutionary explanations ask how a pattern might have arisen or been maintained. Yale’s first emotion lecture takes that perspective on examples including fear and social bonds. The second lecture develops social relationships and reciprocity. These 2007 lectures provide hypotheses to understand and examine, not permission to assume that every plausible story about usefulness is an established evolutionary history.

Measurement determines what a study can conclude. A facial-expression judgement, a physiological recording and a person’s report of their feelings may disagree because they measure different aspects of an episode. Before calling that disagreement a failure, ask whether the proposed theory predicted that all three should move together. Cultural and situational differences also make it necessary to specify whose behaviour is being studied and what the task asks them to do.

The aim is not to choose a favourite theory from a menu. It is to identify which question each theory addresses, what it predicts and what observation could count against it. Explanations at bodily, experiential, interpersonal and evolutionary levels can sometimes complement one another. They compete when they make incompatible predictions about the same observation.

Turn two accounts into different predictions

Use a fictional episode of hearing an unexpected noise. Draw two possible orders linking bodily change, interpretation, feeling and action. Choose two measurements, specify a prediction on which the accounts differ and identify a plausible confound. Do not induce fear in yourself or anyone else; this is a design exercise.

Key terms

Valence
How pleasant or unpleasant an experience is.
Arousal
The degree of activation, distinguished from pleasantness.
Functional explanation
An account of what a process contributes in a specified context, distinct from its immediate mechanism.
