Learning paths

A sequence with a purpose.

Choose an entry route or a deeper course of study. Each step explains why it belongs and gives you a task.

From close bonds to groups and cultural expectations

Begin with defined relationships, add physiological and field perspectives, then examine groups, culturally valued feelings and policy aims. The path’s recurring task is to make each change of scale explicit.

7 steps · Learning path

Comparing minds across species without a single ladder

Move from scaling and task design to development, field behaviour and human origins. Each step changes one part of the comparison so that the final synthesis is a research plan, not a ranking of species.

7 steps · Learning path

From experience to competing theories of consciousness

This sequence first clarifies the target, then contrasts workspace, integrated-information and predictive accounts, and finally examines how measures and models enter an empirical test. It does not promise a settled theory of consciousness.

8 steps · Learning path

Design and evaluate a behavioural study

An eight-step route from a population and outcome to a comparison, power assumptions and an interpretable interval. The associated guide and design readings supply the experimental-design concepts that the statistics demonstrations do not cover.

6 steps · Learning path

Development from behaviour to brain and study design

Compare two introductory accounts of child development, extend the question across adulthood, then move to cortical organisation and the setting in which developmental studies are conducted. The recurring task is to separate a target ability from the demands of its measurement.

→

8 steps · Learning path

Emotion, stress and questions about regulation

Begin with what an emotional episode contains, compare functional and social explanations, and then study stress and regulation as research questions. The route repeatedly asks what was measured and what would count as a change. It does not promise improved wellbeing or teach a treatment package. The final task turns an appealing regulation claim into an evaluable study question.

→

5 steps · Learning path

From a mental-health claim to an evidence question

Move from classification and a specific disorder family to treatment evaluation and the difference between individual and population questions. Read the current NIH/NIMH/NICE companions alongside the older lectures. The final product is a hypothetical evidence table or protocol, not a recommendation about care. Each step asks what would support the claim being made and what remains unexplained.

→

6 steps · Learning path

From a study attempt to a teaching design

Moves from a learner’s retrieval attempt to an educator’s aligned task and feedback design.

8 steps · Learning path

Following a movement system from muscle to brain

Work upwards through the dedicated human-movement course: motor units, spinal circuits and higher systems. The order preserves the relevant official course sequence while omitting unrelated lessons for a manageable first route. Each task adds to one labelled explanation of a hypothetical reach. Anatomical assignments remain tentative until supported; the aim is to connect levels, not memorise a different structure at every stop.

8 steps · Learning path

From membranes to interacting circuits

This route follows the mechanism of communication: charge movement, membrane response, transmission and interacting populations. It differs from a general introduction to computational neuroscience by organising every step around what changes between an input and an output. Begin with conceptual sketches, then use the mathematical and notebook material only as preparation allows. The recurring task is to identify what is measured, what is modelled and what the model omits.

What a score says about a person

Compares performance-based and descriptive measures, then asks what their construction permits us to infer.

8 steps · Learning path

From neurons and spike data to network models

Begin with physical and circuit models, connect them to synapses and spike data, then extend to receptive fields and recurrent memory models. Each step asks what the representation includes and what observation would challenge it. This is a selected route, with the full course available for omitted derivations.

6 steps · Learning path

From a brain question to an experiment

Follow the Human Brain course from its central questions through methods, experimental design and competing interpretations. The route preserves the relevant lecture order and adds a continuing task: refine one question into two discriminating experiments.

10 steps · Learning path

Read a quantitative paper critically

A ten-step reading sequence that moves from the recorded variables and descriptive patterns to the tested parameter, uncertainty, model assumptions and prediction claims. Use the linked causal and transparency readings for questions the statistical calculation cannot answer.

6 steps · Learning path

Sleep, hunger and the circumstances of motivation

Start with Sleep 3, whose official description concerns rhythms and sleep, and the accompanying NIGMS reading; then compare emotion and eating. Sleep 1/2 are omitted from the required sequence because their descriptions discuss consciousness and appear inconsistent with their titles. The route is organised around regulation, cues and measurement, ending with food access so that an explanation of a choice remains connected to its circumstances.

A first route through strategic interaction

Preserves order within Yale’s course while selecting a bridge from simultaneous choice to timing, information and repetition.

7 steps · Learning path

Studying change from adolescence to ageing

Compare two transitions rather than surveying every stage of life. The first half asks how biological change, peers and media settings are studied; the second asks how cognitive outcomes and theories of ageing are distinguished. Repeated tasks separate group differences from within-person change. This route is about the logic and scope of developmental claims, not a timetable for judging individuals.
